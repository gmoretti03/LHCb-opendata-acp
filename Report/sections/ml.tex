\section{Machine Learning Module}
\label{sec:ml}
\emph{In short: classifiers trained on weak labels from the mass spectrum (there are no true labels) reach a test AUC of $0.86$; a cut on their score leaves $\Araw$ unchanged, and an adaptive partition of the Dalitz plane shows the same pattern as the fixed binning.}
As a complementary, data-driven approach to the signal/background separation of the mass fits (Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:bayesian}), supervised classifiers were trained on weak labels derived from the mass spectrum, and used to study whether an ML-assisted event selection changes $\Araw$ or the mass spectrum, and whether an adaptive partition of the Dalitz plane resolves local structure.

\subsection{Weak Labels and Feature Set}
\label{sec:ml_labels}
\emph{In short: a classifier separates a signal-enriched window from the mass sidebands ($15{,}130$ signal-like and $1940$ background-like candidates, $20$ features, $60/20/20$ split); by the Neyman-Pearson argument of the CWoLa approach it is also the signal/background classifier, if the features carry no information on the mass.}
No candidate carries a true signal/background label, so the classifiers are trained with weak labels, following the classification-without-labels (CWoLa) approach~\cite{cwola}: a classifier is trained to separate two \emph{mixed} samples with different signal fractions, here a signal-enriched window around the $B$ peak and the mass sidebands. If, within each class, the feature distributions are the same in the two samples, the optimal classifier between the two mixtures is a monotonic function of the signal-to-background likelihood ratio $p_s(x)/p_b(x)$, which by the Neyman-Pearson lemma is the most powerful test statistic for signal against background: the classifier trained on the mixtures is also the optimal signal/background classifier~\cite{cwola}. Two conditions follow. The background in the sidebands must be representative of the background under the peak; and the features must not be correlated with the $B$ mass. The second condition cannot hold exactly: the reconstructed mass is not an input feature, but the nine momentum components of the three kaons determine it, so a flexible model can partly learn the mass, separate the two mass regions rather than signal from background, and \emph{sculpt} the mass distribution of the candidates that pass a cut on its score. Section~\ref{sec:ml_stability} tests this directly.

The labels use the nominal Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}, $\mu=5284.39$, $\sigma=18.00\,\MeV$). Candidates with $|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $m\in[5248.4,5320.4]\,\MeV$, are labeled signal-like ($15{,}130$); candidates in $[5150,\mu-4\sigma]\cup[\mu+4\sigma,5600]=[5150,5212.4]\cup[5356.4,5600]\,\MeV$ are labeled background-like ($1940$); the $2$--$4\sigma$ gap is left unlabeled. The region $5100$--$5150\,\MeV$, inside the fit window, is excluded from the background-like class to satisfy the first condition: there the background rises towards partially reconstructed $B$ decays (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_mass}), whose kinematics differ from those of the combinatorial background under the peak. The classes are imbalanced ($1940/15{,}130=0.128$). Every model is trained with the same balanced class weights, $w_c=N/(2N_c)$, i.e.\ $4.40$ for background-like and $0.56$ for signal-like candidates (through \texttt{class\_weight} for the scikit-learn models and the network, and through sample weights for gradient boosting), so that the two classes contribute equally to the loss. (Down-sampling the signal-like class and up-weighting it afterwards would have the same effect with less training data; the weights keep all candidates.)

The feature set consists of the 6 per-track kinematic/PID variables (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}, \texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}, \texttt{IPChi2}) for each of the 3 final-state tracks (18 features), plus two $B$-candidate variables not used in the preselection: \texttt{B\_FlightDistance} and \texttt{B\_VertexChi2}, standard discriminants against combinatorial background (a genuine $B$ flies further before decaying and has a better-reconstructed vertex than a random track combination). Both separate the weak-label classes (Kolmogorov-Smirnov $p<10^{-60}$) and are not redundant with the per-track features (Spearman $|\rho|\leq0.53$ with each of the 18), giving $20$ features. A principal-component analysis of the standardized features needs $15$ of $20$ components to reach $90\%$ of the variance, so no dimensionality reduction is applied; with $20$ features and $\sim10^4$ training candidates the sample is not in the regime where the distance-based methods suffer from the curse of dimensionality. The labeled sample was split $60/20/20$ into training, validation and test sets ($10{,}242/3414/3414$ candidates, $388$ background-like in the test set), stratified on the label, with the feature scaler fit on the training set only.

\subsection{Stability of $\Araw$ under ML Selection}
\label{sec:ml_stability}
\emph{In short: a cut on the score at $0.5$ keeps $92\%$ of the signal and $36\%$ of the background and leaves $\Araw$ within its expected statistical spread with both fits and with counting; it does not improve the precision and cannot remove the misidentified background.}

The reference (gradient-boosting) score was applied to all candidates of the fit window (on the test set, a cut at $0.5$ keeps $88\%$ of the signal-like and $28\%$ of the background-like candidates, Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_roc} of Appendix~\ref{app:ext_ml}), not only to the labeled ones used in training, and the counting asymmetry was recomputed after cuts on the score from $0.1$ to $0.9$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_threshold} of Appendix~\ref{app:ext_ml}). Since every selected sample is contained in the full one, the expected statistical spread of the difference is $\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{sub}}^2-\sigma_{\mathrm{full}}^2}$, not the uncertainty of either estimate. Over all thresholds, which keep from $18{,}443$ to $2621$ candidates, the shift with respect to the full window ($A_{\mathrm{count}}=-0.0432$) stays within $1.4$ times this spread: the selection does not introduce a detectable bias in the counting asymmetry. Split by magnet polarity at a cut of $0.5$, the counting asymmetries are $-0.060\pm0.010$ (down) and $-0.024\pm0.012$ (up), $2.2\sigma$ apart; the frequentist fit of the same samples, which separates the background, gives $-0.054\pm0.011$ and $-0.032\pm0.013$, $1.3\sigma$ apart (against $0.7\sigma$ for the full sample, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}), so most of the larger difference of the counting estimates comes from the background.

Counting cannot reveal whether the selection deforms the mass spectrum. To test this, the selected sample (score $>0.5$, $15{,}709$ candidates) was refitted with the nominal mass model by both inference methods of this work, the Bayesian fit of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayesian} and the frequentist maximum-likelihood fit of Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}, and the three estimates were compared with those of the full sample (Table~\ref{tab:three_methods}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_three}). The asymmetry is stable: all three shifts are within their expected nested-sample spread, and the significance of $A\neq0$ from the profile likelihood (Wilks' theorem~\cite{wilks}) is $5.2\sigma$ in both samples. The selection keeps $92\%$ of the fitted signal and $36\%$ of the background ($N_s=14{,}121$, $N_b=1590$), but this does not improve the precision on $\Araw$ ($0.0087$ against $0.0085$): the background under the peak is already small ($\approx$4\% in the signal window) and the fit separates it from the signal anyway. The mass spectrum, instead, changes. The goodness of fit of the nominal model degrades from $\chi^2=164/88$ to $253/88$ in the Bayesian fit and from $442/388$ to $557/388$ in the frequentist one (with a different binning), and the background shape changes: the slope of the slow exponential grows from $0.0028$ to $0.0071\,\MeV^{-1}$ ($B^+$) and from $0.0016$ to $0.0057\,\MeV^{-1}$ ($B^-$), and the fraction of the steep one from $0.11$ to $0.28$ and from $0.21$ to $0.50$. Two mechanisms can produce this: the classifier could reconstruct the mass from the kaon momenta and sculpt the background (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}), or the selection could change the composition of the background. The first is excluded: the same classifier trained without the nine momentum components (test AUC $0.860$) gives the same degradation ($\chi^2=533/388$). The efficiency of the cut as a function of the mass shows the second (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_mass_cut} of Appendix~\ref{app:ext_ml}): it is $0.89$--$0.90$ at the peak, $\approx0.70$ below $5150\,\MeV$ and $0.06$--$0.13$ above $5375\,\MeV$. The classifier does what it is trained for -- it removes the combinatorial background and keeps genuine $B$ decays, including the partially reconstructed ones and the other non-combinatorial components that the weak labels cannot separate from the signal. In the selected sample these components weigh relatively more, and the nominal model, which has no dedicated component for them, describes it worse, mostly under the peak (the $\chi^2$ of the $\pm4\sigma$ region grows from $75$ to $134$ for 30 bins) and below it. A Crystal Ball signal gives the same degradation ($407\to522$ for 386 degrees of freedom), so it is not due to the signal tails. $\Araw$ is unaffected because it is determined by the peak and the background shape is free in the fit, but a measurement that relied on the background shape, or on a background model taken from the sidebands of the full sample, would be biased by the selection.

\paragraph*{Further material} is in Appendix~\ref{app:ext_ml}. \emph{Model Comparison.} six classifiers; the tree ensembles and the neural network reach test AUC $0.857$--$0.864$, about $0.09$ above the linear models and indistinguishable from each other within the test-set uncertainty ($0.012$); gradient boosting, best in cross-validation, is the reference. \emph{Regularization, Bias-Variance, and Calibration.} the linear model does not overfit, gradient boosting overfits beyond $\sim$150 stages without losing test AUC, and class weights bias the probabilities, which an isotonic recalibration corrects (Brier score $0.122\to0.062$) without changing the ranking. \emph{Uncertainty Quantification.} the three estimators give mean per-candidate uncertainties of $0.178$ (Random Forest), $0.095$ (MC dropout) and $0.050$ (deep ensemble); they measure different sources of variability, and only the Random Forest spread visibly decreases with the training-set size. \emph{K-means on the Dalitz Plot.} for $K=8$ two clusters deviate by more than $3\sigma$ ($\chi^2=46.4$ for $7$ degrees of freedom): a negative asymmetry at low $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ and positive ones at high $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$, as in the fixed binning. \emph{Hyperparameter Optimization.} the best configurations improve the test AUC by at most $0.005$, less than its uncertainty, and the cross-validated maximum overestimates the test AUC (winner's curse). \emph{High-Background Sample.} with a looser identification ($33{,}328$ candidates, purity $94\%$) the classifier raises the purity to $99\%$ and $\sigma(\Araw)$ from $0.0072$ to $0.0070$ at best, but cannot remove the misidentified background that moves $\Araw$ to $-0.055$.
