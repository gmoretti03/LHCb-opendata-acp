\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
\emph{In short: two inference frameworks give the same raw asymmetry, $\Araw=-0.044\pm0.009\,(\text{stat})\pm0.007\,(\text{syst})$, $5.2$ standard deviations from zero, with validated intervals; what separates them is the meaning of the numbers and the tests, not their values; a machine-learning selection (Appendix~\ref{sec:ml}) confirms the result without improving it.}

We have measured the raw charge asymmetry of $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ decays in the 2011 LHCb Open Data record with a common selection and mass model, and compared how two inference frameworks treat the same question. The frequentist profile-likelihood fit and the Bayesian posterior give $\Araw=-0.0441\pm0.0085\pm0.0072$ and $-0.0443\pm0.0085\pm0.0070$ (statistical, systematic), a nonzero asymmetry at $5.2$ standard deviations, stable under the thirteen variants of the fit and selection, compatible in sign and size with the published analysis, and concentrated in its Dalitz region outside the $\phi(1020)$. It is a raw asymmetry, not a measurement of $\ACP$ and not a significance of CP violation (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison_published}).

The two methods differ in what they add. The frequentist fit gives intervals with the nominal coverage ($68.4\pm1.5\%$ in $1000$ bootstrap replicas, $0.70\pm0.01$ in toy MC) and an unbiased estimator that reaches the minimum variance bound, with Hesse and Minos in agreement. The Bayesian analysis gives the same interval and coverage with flat priors, and adds sampling diagnostics that must be checked, a Bayes factor that depends on the prior width, and a posterior predictive check whose charge statistics, being fitted, have no power. A machine-learning selection trained on weak labels (Appendix~\ref{sec:ml}) leaves $\Araw$ unchanged and cannot remove the misidentified decays under the signal.

The dominant systematic contribution is the particle-identification requirement, consistent with a statistical fluctuation of a nested sample, and the same variants and rule give the same uncertainty in the two methods; several statistics depend on choices (the binned $\chi^2$ on the binning, the Bayes factor on the prior).

Three limitations bound the result (Appendix~\ref{sec:limitations}): no tested model reaches $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}\approx1$, although $\Araw$ does not depend on the signal shape; no production or detection correction is applied, so $\Araw\neq\ACP$; and the local Dalitz-plane asymmetries are counts without a background subtraction by event weights.
