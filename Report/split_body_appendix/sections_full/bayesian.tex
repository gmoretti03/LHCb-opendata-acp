\section{Bayesian Analysis}
\label{sec:bayesian}

\subsection{Priors and Posterior}
\label{sec:bayes_prior}
\emph{In short: uniform bounded priors on twelve parameters; with $\sim2\times10^4$ candidates the posterior is dominated by the likelihood.}
Bayes' theorem combines the likelihood of Eq.~\eqref{eq:likelihood} (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}) with a prior $\pi(\theta)$ into the posterior
\begin{equation}
p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{p(D)},
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
The priors are uniform, with independent ranges except for the ordering of the background slopes: $N_s,N_b\in(0,5N)$ with $N=19{,}731$, $A,A_b\in(-1,1)$, $\mu\in(5260,5310)\,\MeV$, $\sigma\in(3,40)\,\MeV$, $\lambda_1^{\pm}\in(10^{-5},0.1)\,\MeV^{-1}$, $\lambda_2^{\pm}\in(10^{-5},\lambda_1^{\pm})$, $f_{\pm}\in(0,1)$. The prior density is therefore a constant on the allowed region $\Theta$,
\begin{equation}
\pi(\theta)\propto\mathbf{1}\left[\theta\in\Theta\right],
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
and inside it the posterior is proportional to the likelihood. A flat prior is not uninformative in an absolute sense, since it is not invariant under reparametrization (a prior uniform in $\lambda$ is not uniform in $1/\lambda$); with $\sim$$2\times10^4$ events, however, the posterior is dominated by the likelihood, as the prior-sensitivity check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks} confirms for $A$. The evidence $p(D)$ is a constant normalization: the MCMC samples $\mathcal{L}\,\pi$ without computing it, and the Savage-Dickey ratio of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} avoids computing it as well. The marginal posterior of the asymmetry, $p(A\mid D)=\int p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta_{\neq A}$, is the histogram of the $A$ component of the chain.

\subsection{MCMC Sampling and Convergence Diagnostics}
\label{sec:bayes_mcmc}
\emph{In short: 32 walkers for $25{,}000$ steps; the chain is at least $113$ autocorrelation times long for every parameter and independent runs from dispersed starting points agree.}
The posterior was sampled with the affine-invariant ensemble sampler of Goodman and Weare~\cite{goodman_weare} as implemented in \texttt{emcee}~\cite{emcee}. The maximum of the posterior was first located numerically (Nelder-Mead), and 32 walkers were initialized in a narrow ball around it and run for $25{,}000$ steps, with the first $4166$ ($1/6$) discarded as burn-in, leaving $6.7\times10^5$ samples; the mean acceptance fraction is $0.38$. Convergence was checked through the integrated autocorrelation time of each parameter, $\tau=1+2\sum_{t\geq1}\rho(t)$, with $\rho(t)$ the autocorrelation of the chain at lag $t$. We find $\tau\in[147,183]$ steps, the longest for the background-shape parameters: the post-burn-in chain is $N/\tau\geq113$ autocorrelation times long for every parameter, above the threshold of $50$ recommended for a reliable estimate of $\tau$~\cite{emcee}, and corresponds to $\gtrsim3600$ effectively independent samples per parameter. To check that the chain is not trapped near its starting point, the fit was repeated twice with the walkers initialized uniformly over the whole prior box and the first half of the chain discarded: both runs converge to the same single mode, with $\Araw=-0.0446$ and $-0.0442$, the same intervals and the same values of all other parameters as the nominal fit. The trace plot of such a run (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_trace}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}) shows all walkers reaching the bulk of the posterior within $\sim$2000 steps, the background fraction $f_+$ last, well inside the discarded half of the chain.

\subsection{Posterior Results and Credible Intervals}
\label{sec:bayes_posterior}
\emph{In short: $\Araw=-0.0443$, 68\% credible interval $[-0.0528,-0.0358]$ and 95\% $[-0.0608,-0.0276]$; the posterior is Gaussian.}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_corner.pdf}
\caption{Posterior of the six physics parameters of the nominal fit (the six background-shape parameters are marginalized). Diagonal: marginal posteriors with the 16th, 50th and 84th percentiles (dashed lines and titles). Off-diagonal: 2D marginals, contours enclosing the regions of the $1\sigma$ and $2\sigma$ levels of a 2D Gaussian ($39\%$ and $86\%$).}
\label{fig:bayes_corner}
\end{figure*}

The marginal posterior on the raw asymmetry gives (median and equal-tailed credible intervals, CI)
\begin{gather*}
\Araw = -0.0443, \\
68\%~\text{CI}~[-0.0528,-0.0358], \\ 95\%~\text{CI}~[-0.0608,-0.0276].
\end{gather*}
The posterior is very close to Gaussian, with standard deviation $0.0085$; its 68\% interval is symmetric about the median to within $2\times10^{-5}$, so the equal-tailed intervals coincide with the highest-posterior-density ones at the quoted precision. Since the priors are uniform, the posterior mode (the maximum a posteriori, MAP, estimate, $-0.0441$) coincides with the maximum-likelihood estimate, so this result can be compared directly with the frequentist fit of the same model (Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}; the quantities it uses are summarized in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_freq}). The published analysis of the same 2011 data, with its own selection (which includes a $D^0$ veto) and a mass model with partially reconstructed and misidentified backgrounds, finds $\Araw=-0.060\pm0.007$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}; with the $D^0$ veto our fit gives $-0.0496\pm0.0088$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}), compatible within the statistical uncertainties, although the two selections overlap and differ. After the corrections of Eq.~\eqref{eq:acp}, the published asymmetry is $\ACP=-0.043\pm0.009\pm0.003\pm0.007$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}, and the most precise later measurement, on a larger data set, is $\ACP=-0.037\pm0.002\pm0.002\pm0.003$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023,lhcb_cpv_paper}. The other physics parameters are $N_s=15{,}328\pm133$, $N_b=4404\pm82$, $A_b=-0.040\pm0.018$, $\mu=5284.39\pm0.16\,\MeV$ and $\sigma=18.00\pm0.14\,\MeV$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_corner}). $A$ is almost uncorrelated with the other parameters: its largest correlation, $-0.17$, is with $A_b$: conditioning on $A_b=0$ shifts $A$ by $\rho\,\sigma_A/\sigma_{A_b}\,(0-\bar A_b)=-0.0033$, to $-0.0475$, which is what the fit with a charge-symmetric background gives ($-0.0471$, Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}).



\subsection{Bayes Factors}
\label{sec:bayes_factor}
\emph{In short: decisive evidence against $A=0$ (Bayes factor $BF_{10}\approx9\times10^3$); the evidence for a background asymmetry is not significant, and depends on the prior width.}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_savage_dickey.pdf}
\caption{Savage-Dickey ratio for (a) $A$ and (b) $A_b$: marginal posterior (histogram of the chain and Gaussian approximation) and prior density, logarithmic scale. The Bayes factor $BF_{01}$ is the ratio of the two densities at zero (dots). For $A$ the posterior density at zero is $10^{-4}$ times the prior one; for $A_b$, $2.3$ standard deviations from zero, it is higher than the prior density, and the data favor $A_b=0$.}
\label{fig:bayes_sd}
\end{figure*}

The Bayes factor compares two hypotheses through their evidences (marginal likelihoods), $BF_{10}=P(D\mid H_1)/P(D\mid H_0)$ with $P(D\mid H_i)=\int\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta,H_i)\,\pi(\theta\mid H_i)\,d\theta$: it is the factor by which the data update the prior odds of the two hypotheses, and it requires proper priors. Computing the evidence of each hypothesis by direct numerical integration is impractical for a 12-parameter posterior. When the null hypothesis fixes one parameter, $H_0$: $A=0$, nested in $H_1$ ($A$ free), and the priors on the other parameters are shared, the Bayes factor reduces to the Savage-Dickey density ratio~\cite{dickey1971} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_freq}),
\[
BF_{01} = \frac{p(A=0\mid D)}{\pi(A=0)}, \qquad \pi(A=0)=\tfrac12 .
\]
With equal prior probabilities for the two hypotheses, $BF_{01}$ is also the posterior odds of $H_0$ against $H_1$. $A=0$ lies $5.2$ posterior standard deviations from the median, in a tail where the chain contains essentially no samples, so a kernel density estimate of $p(A=0\mid D)$ would only reflect the kernel tails of the few most extreme samples. We therefore use the Gaussian approximation of the marginal posterior (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}), with the mean and standard deviation of the chain, which gives $BF_{01}\approx1.1\times10^{-4}$ ($BF_{10}\approx9\times10^{3}$, $\ln BF_{10}\approx9.1$): decisive evidence against $A=0$ on Jeffreys' scale~\cite{jeffreys} ($BF_{10}>100$). As for any Bayes factor of a point null against a free parameter, the value scales with the prior width -- the Ockham factor built into the Bayes factor, which penalizes a free parameter in proportion to the prior volume it is allowed to explore, and the mechanism behind Lindley's paradox: with the narrower prior $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}, $\pi(A=0)$ grows to $1/0.6$ and $BF_{01}$ drops by a factor $3.3$, which leaves the conclusion unchanged. Figure~\ref{fig:bayes_sd} shows the two densities of the ratio; the dependence on the prior width, the evidence computed by direct integration and the equality of the background shapes of the two charges are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_checks}.

The same test on the background asymmetry shows the Ockham factor at work. $A_b=-0.040\pm0.018$ lies $2.3$ standard deviations from zero, yet the Savage-Dickey ratio gives $BF_{01}(A_b=0)\approx3.5$: the data mildly \emph{favor} a charge-symmetric background, because the gain in likelihood does not compensate for spreading the prior of $A_b$ over $(-1,1)$. A negative background asymmetry is plausible: besides combinatorial background, the sample contains misidentified $B^\pm\to K^\pm\pi^+\pi^-$ and $B^\pm\to\pi^\pm K^+K^-$ decays, with $\ACP$ values of their own, whose contribution grows when the kaon identification is loosened (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}). $A_b$ is nevertheless kept free in the nominal model, which avoids imposing an assumption on the background that the frequentist analysis does not make; fixing $A_b=0$ is one of the variants of the systematic study (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}).



\subsection{Model Adequacy: Goodness of Fit and Posterior Predictive Check}
\label{sec:bayes_gof}
\emph{In short: the model does not reach $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}\approx1$ ($164.1/88$), mainly because the signal tails are heavier than a Gaussian, but $\Araw$ changes by only $-0.0002$ with a Crystal Ball signal.}

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{$\Araw$ (posterior median) and goodness of fit of the nominal model (Gaussian signal, double exponential per charge) and of the alternative signal and background models, same data and window.}
\label{tab:gof}
\end{table}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_mass_fit.pdf}
\caption{Mass distributions of (a) $B^+$ and (b) $B^-$ candidates with the nominal model (posterior medians, logarithmic scale): total, signal and background components. Bottom: pulls, $(\text{obs}-\text{exp})/\sqrt{\text{exp}}$.}
\label{fig:bayes_fit}
\end{figure*}

A binned comparison of data and posterior-median model ($\chi^2=\sum(\text{obs}-\text{exp})^2/\text{exp}$ over 50 bins of $10\,\MeV$ per charge, $100-12=88$ degrees of freedom) gives $\chi^2=164.1/88$ ($p=1.6\times10^{-6}$), with $95.4$ for $B^+$ and $68.7$ for $B^-$. The largest pulls, in the $B^+$ sample, sit on the two flanks of the peak, about $\pm3\sigma$ from $\mu$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_fit}): the observed signal shape has heavier tails than a Gaussian. Replacing the Gaussian by a Crystal Ball shape~\cite{skwarnicki}, a Gaussian core with a power-law low-mass tail,
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{CB}}(x)\propto
\begin{cases}
e^{-x^2/2}, & x>-\beta,\\[2pt]
\left(\frac{\kappa}{\beta}\right)^{\kappa} e^{-\beta^2/2}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\beta}-\beta-x\right)^{-\kappa}, & x\leq-\beta,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:cb}
\end{equation}
with $x=(m-\mu)/\sigma$, $\beta$ the point where the tail starts (in units of $\sigma$) and $\kappa$ its power, both free, improves the fit to $\chi^2=131.0/86$ ($p=1.3\times10^{-3}$, $\beta=1.71$, $\kappa=35$) but moves $\Araw$ by only $-0.0002$ (Table~\ref{tab:gof}). The asymmetry does not depend on the signal shape even though none of the models reaches $\chi^2/\text{ndof}\approx1$. The Gaussian is nevertheless kept as the nominal signal model, common to the two analyses: the Crystal Ball adds two tail parameters that are poorly constrained (its power $\kappa$ runs to large values), and the effect of the signal shape on $\Araw$ is covered by the systematic study (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}).

The background model is constrained by the data much more strongly. A single exponential per charge is rejected ($\chi^2=297.7/92$, $\Delta\chi^2=134$ for four fewer parameters), and so it is not used even as a systematic variant (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}). A shape shared between the two charges ($\Delta\chi^2=10.0$ for three fewer parameters) and a charge-symmetric background ($\Delta\chi^2=5.7$ for one fewer) are acceptable descriptions and shift $\Araw$ by $-0.0037$ and $-0.0028$: the background treatment is the largest model dependence of the fit. The lower edge of the window is a compromise between two failures. Extending the window to $5050\,\MeV$ degrades the fit ($\chi^2=217.4/98$), because the double exponential no longer follows the background further below the peak; raising it to $5150\,\MeV$ removes most of the region that constrains the steep component, which becomes poorly identified -- the chain for its fraction $f_+$ mixes slowly ($N/\tau=10$) -- while $\Araw$ itself is unchanged ($-0.0444$).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_ppc.pdf}
\caption{Posterior predictive check: distributions of three discrepancies over $1000$ datasets replicated from posterior draws, compared with the observed values. (a) binned $\chi^2$; (b) and (c) counting asymmetry of the fit window and of the sidebands.}
\label{fig:bayes_ppc}
\end{figure*}

The binned $\chi^2$ uses the posterior medians only. A posterior predictive check~\cite{gelman_ppc} accounts for the posterior uncertainty and tests specific features of the data: for $1000$ parameter sets $\theta_j$ drawn from the posterior, a dataset $y^{\mathrm{rep}}_j$ is generated from the model, and a discrepancy $T(y,\theta_j)$ is computed both on the replica and on the data; the posterior predictive $p$-value is $p_B=P\big(T(y^{\mathrm{rep}},\theta)\geq T(y,\theta)\mid y\big)$. For the binned $\chi^2$ the replicas give $104\pm15$ and the data $175$ (median over $\theta_j$): none of the replicas reaches the observed value ($p_B<10^{-3}$), with the misfit concentrated in the $B^+$ sample ($p_B<10^{-3}$, against $0.03$ for $B^-$). The counting asymmetries of the whole window ($p_B=0.996$) and of the signal window ($0.99$) are reproduced, but this is expected and not informative: $A$ is a free parameter, so the replicas are centred on the observed value by construction. The asymmetry of the sidebands ($0.73$), which the fit constrains less directly, is also reproduced (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_ppc}). The check therefore shows that the model misdescribes the shape of the spectrum, mainly the signal tails, and does not reveal a charge-dependent distortion of the sidebands; that $\Araw$ does not depend on the signal shape is shown directly by its stability under the Crystal Ball model (Table~\ref{tab:gof}).







\subsection{Robustness Checks: Polarity, Prior and Charm Vetoes}
\label{sec:bayes_checks}
\emph{In short: $\Araw$ agrees between the two magnet polarities ($0.7\sigma$) and does not depend on the prior; the $D^0$ veto moves it by $2.3$ times the expected statistical spread.}
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_magnet.pdf}
\caption{Posterior of $\Araw$ for the magnet-down, magnet-up and combined samples.}
\label{fig:bayes_magnet}
\end{figure}

The fit was repeated separately on the two magnet polarities, whose left-right detection asymmetries are opposite and partially cancel in the combined sample: $\Araw(\text{down})=-0.0497\pm0.0112$ ($n=11{,}636$) and $\Araw(\text{up})=-0.0378\pm0.0129$ ($n=8095$); the frequentist fits give $-0.0492\pm0.0111$ and $-0.0374\pm0.0131$, and the sum of their profile likelihoods $-0.0443\pm0.0084$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_checks}). They differ by $0.7\sigma$, so there is no indication that the asymmetry is an artifact of one polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_magnet}). A narrower prior, $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$, leaves the posterior unchanged ($\Araw=-0.0443$), as expected for a posterior far from the prior bounds; the prior width matters only for the Bayes factor (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor}).



The sample contains $B^\pm$ decays through intermediate charm states, $B^\pm\to D^0K^\pm$, $J/\psi K^\pm$ and $\chi_{c0}K^\pm$ with $D^0$, $J/\psi$, $\chi_{c0}\to K^+K^-$, which are visible as peaks in the opposite-sign $m(K^+K^-)$ spectra. They are part of the $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ final state and are kept in the nominal sample; removing them changes the observable, from the asymmetry of all $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ decays to that of the charmless ones, so the vetoes are reported as an informative check rather than as a systematic variation. A candidate is vetoed if either opposite-sign pair, $m(H1,H2)$ or $m(H1,H3)$, lies within $\pm30\,\MeV$ of the $D^0$ or $J/\psi$ mass or within $\pm60\,\MeV$ of the $\chi_{c0}$ mass (world-average masses; the wider window for the $\chi_{c0}$ reflects its natural width). The $D^0$ veto alone keeps $18{,}744$ candidates and gives $\Araw=-0.0496\pm0.0088$; the three vetoes together keep $16{,}367$ and give $-0.0423\pm0.0094$. The shifts with respect to the nominal fit, $-0.0053$ and $+0.0020$, compare with expected statistical fluctuations of $0.0023$ and $0.0040$ for a subsample of the nominal one (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}); the $D^0$ veto moves $\Araw$ by $2.3$ times this amount, i.e.\ the vetoed $D^0K^\pm$ candidates have a less negative asymmetry than the rest of the sample. The analysis was also carried out with a minimal six-parameter model, with a narrower window and a single exponential background, before the nominal model was fixed; it gives a compatible result (Appendix~\ref{sec:bayes_minimal}).

\subsection{Toy-MC Coverage Validation}
\label{sec:bayes_toymc}
\emph{In short: $200$ pseudo-experiments give coverage $0.65\pm0.03$ ($68\%$) and $0.93\pm0.02$ ($95\%$), consistent with nominal, with unbiased estimates and pulls of width $1.04$.}
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/bayes_nominal_toys.pdf}
\caption{Toy-MC validation, 200 pseudo-datasets. (a) Pulls $(\hat A-A_{\mathrm{true}})/\sigma(\hat A)$, with $\hat A$ and $\sigma(\hat A)$ the posterior median and standard deviation of each replica, compared with a standard normal. (b) Fraction of replicas whose 68\% and 95\% credible intervals contain the injected value, with binomial uncertainties, and the nominal coverage.}
\label{fig:bayes_toys}
\end{figure}

The two kinds of interval have different meanings. A Bayesian credible interval is a statement about the parameter given the observed data and the prior: the true $A$ lies in it with the stated probability. A frequentist confidence interval, such as the profile-likelihood interval of Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}, is obtained by inverting a hypothesis test: the parameter is fixed and the interval is the random quantity, which changes from one repetition of the experiment to the next, and the confidence level is the fraction of repetitions in which it contains the true value (its coverage); it is not the probability that the true value lies in the interval observed. With flat priors and $\sim2\times10^4$ events the two intervals coincide numerically (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}), but only the confidence interval has the nominal coverage by construction, and only asymptotically; nothing guarantees it for the credible interval. To check this, 200 pseudo-datasets were generated from the fitted model (parametric bootstrap, with the posterior medians as ground truth and Poisson-fluctuated yields for each charge and component; the truncated Gaussian and exponential masses are drawn with the transformation method, i.e.\ by inverting their cumulative distributions) and each was refit with the nominal procedure and a $12{,}000$-step chain. The fraction of replicas whose interval contains the injected $A$ is $0.650\pm0.033$ for the 68\% CI and $0.930\pm0.015$ for the 95\% CI (binomial uncertainties), $1.0\sigma$ and $1.3\sigma$ below nominal: the coverage is consistent with nominal. The estimates themselves are unbiased and their spread matches the reported uncertainty (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_toys}): their mean differs from the injected value by $-0.0002\pm0.0006$, their standard deviation, $0.0088$, agrees with the posterior standard deviation of the data fit, $0.0085$, and the pulls have mean $-0.03\pm0.07$ and standard deviation $1.04$.

The toy chains are shorter than the nominal one, and in 124 of the 200 replicas the slowest-mixing parameter, always a background-shape parameter, has $N/\tau<50$ (median $48$, minimum $22$). The criterion on the slowest parameter is conservative for the quantity tested here: for $A$ itself the median autocorrelation time is $156$ steps, so each chain is at least $45$ autocorrelation times long in $A$ and provides $\gtrsim1400$ effectively independent samples of it. The Monte Carlo error on the percentiles of $A$ is then a few per cent of $\sigma(A)$, negligible against the binomial uncertainty of the coverage, and the replicas with $N/\tau<50$ do not show a lower coverage than the others ($0.69$ for the $68\%$ interval, against $0.58\pm0.06$ for the $76$ replicas with $N/\tau\geq50$, whose coverage is $1.9$ standard deviations below nominal: the split was made after the fact and $200$ replicas cannot tell a trend from a fluctuation, and the check on the data of Sec.~\ref{sec:freq_coverage} gives the nominal coverage). This is a closure test of the inference procedure: since the toys are generated from the same model that is fitted, it does not probe the model mismatch of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}, which is instead addressed by the systematic study.
