\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Charge-parity (CP) violation -- the small observed asymmetry between the behavior of matter and antimatter -- is one of the necessary ingredients for the matter-dominated universe we observe, yet the CP violation built into the Standard Model is far too small to account for it. Studying CP violation in the decays of $B$ mesons, where it is comparatively large and experimentally accessible, is one of the main ways to test the Standard Model's CKM description of the effect and search for physics beyond it. Charmless three-body decays such as $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ ($h=K,\pi$) are especially interesting in this respect: unlike a two-body decay, the three-body phase space (the Dalitz plot) allows the raw asymmetry $\Araw$ to be studied not only as a single global number, but also \emph{locally}, since interference between intermediate resonances can concentrate CP-violating effects in specific kinematic regions rather than spreading them uniformly~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023,lhcb_cpv_paper}.

In this work we use the LHCb Open Data record for $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$~\cite{opendata_record,opendata_notebook} to address the question: \emph{to what extent do frequentist likelihood fits, Bayesian inference, and machine-learning-assisted event selection produce consistent, well-controlled estimates of the raw asymmetry $\Araw$ in this decay?} The aim is not a competitive measurement of $\ACP$, but a methodological comparison of the three inference frameworks on the same data and the same physical question. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset and the preselection common to the three analyses; Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:ml} present the frequentist, Bayesian, and machine-learning analyses respectively; Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} brings the three together.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset and Preselection}
\label{sec:data_presel}
The dataset is the CERN Open Data record 4900, ``Matter Antimatter Differences -- B meson decays to three hadrons''~\cite{opendata_record}: candidates for $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ decays collected by LHCb in 2011 at $\sqrt{s}=7$\,TeV, reconstructed with the LHCb software and already passed through trigger and offline selection. The data are distributed as ROOT \texttt{TTree}s (\texttt{DecayTree}) containing only the most relevant reconstructed branches, split across two files by magnet polarity (magnet up/down), for a combined $8{,}556{,}118$ candidates. The same record also includes a simplified, detector-response-free phase-space simulation of $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$; it is not used in this work.

For each of the three final-state tracks ($H1$, $H2$, $H3$) the analysis reads momentum components (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}), PID likelihoods (\texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}), a muon flag (\texttt{isMuon}), and the track charge -- 18 kinematic/PID branches plus 3 charges. The same preselection is applied in all three analyses (frequentist, Bayesian, machine learning), so that their results are directly comparable: a kaon-PID cut and a muon veto applied to all three tracks,
\[
\texttt{H*\_ProbPi} < 0.5 \ \wedge\ \texttt{H*\_ProbK} > 0.5 \ \wedge\ \lnot\texttt{H*\_isMuon},
\]
which retains $23{,}895$ of the $8{,}556{,}118$ read candidates ($0.28\%$) -- the PID requirement, applied on all three tracks simultaneously, is by far the dominant reduction, consistent with selecting a comparatively pure $KKK$ final state out of a much larger, PID-unconstrained sample.

In this dataset $H1$ is, by construction, always the track of opposite charge to the other two: only the charge combinations $(+1,-1,-1)$ and $(-1,+1,+1)$ occur for $(H1,H2,H3)$. Equivalently, $H2$ and $H3$ are always the same-sign, interchangeable pair (the two kaons sharing the sign of the $B$), while $H1$ is the unique kaon of opposite sign -- a labeling convention that matters when constructing the Dalitz-plot variables, which are the invariant masses of the two opposite-sign pairs, $m^2(H1,H2)$ and $m^2(H1,H3)$.

\subsection{Invariant Mass Reconstruction}
\label{sec:data_mass}
The three final-state tracks are assigned the charged-kaon mass hypothesis ($M_K=493.677\,\MeV$) regardless of their PID likelihoods, and their four-momenta are summed to reconstruct the $B$ candidate, $m(KKK)=\sqrt{(\sum_i E_i)^2-|\sum_i \vec{p}_i|^2}$. Below $\sim$5150\,\MeV\ the background rises, consistent with a partially reconstructed contribution that a single exponential cannot describe; the fit window used throughout -- in the frequentist and Bayesian fits and to build the ML weak labels (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}) -- is therefore $[5150,5450]\,\MeV$. This leaves $17{,}839$ preselected candidates in the fit window: $9358$ with $B$-charge $+1$ and $8481$ with $B$-charge $-1$, split $10{,}500$ (magnet down) / $7339$ (magnet up).

\subsection{Data Exploration}
\label{sec:data_exploration}
Figure~\ref{fig:mass_spectrum} shows the reconstructed mass spectrum in the fit window, split by $B$ charge (left) and by magnet polarity (right). The spectrum peaks at $m\approx5284\,\MeV$ with a visible signal excess over a falling combinatorial background, and its shape is qualitatively similar for the two charges and the two magnet polarities -- as expected, since a large raw asymmetry would already be visible by eye at this level. The simple charge-counting asymmetry on the full fit-window sample, $A_{\mathrm{count}}=(N^--N^+)/(N^-+N^+)=-0.049$, is numerically close to all the fit-based estimates below (Secs.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}, \ref{sec:ml_stability}). Since $A_{\mathrm{count}}$ mixes signal and background, this closeness is itself informative: with a charge-symmetric background one would expect $A_{\mathrm{count}}\approx\Araw\,N_s/(N_s+N_b)\approx-0.043$, so the data suggest a background asymmetry of similar sign and size to the signal one (see the sideband check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}).

\section{Bayesian Analysis}
\label{sec:bayesian}

\subsection{Model and Priors}
\label{sec:bayes_model}
The Bayesian analysis reuses the same physical mass model as the frequentist fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}): a signal-plus-background mixture
\[
f(m) = N_s\, S(m;\mu,\sigma) + N_b\, B(m;\lambda),
\]
with $S$ a truncated Gaussian and $B$ a truncated exponential, both normalized on the fit window $[5150,5450]\,\MeV$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_mass}). The signal yield is split between the two $B$-meson charges through a simultaneous parametrization, $N_s^+ = N_s(1-A)/2$ and $N_s^- = N_s(1+A)/2$, so that $A$ is a fit parameter rather than being derived from two independent yields; the background is assumed to be shared equally between charges, $N_b^{\pm}=N_b/2$. The likelihood is the extended unbinned likelihood of both charge samples, Eq.~\eqref{eq:likelihood}.

The full parameter vector is $\theta=(N_s,N_b,A,\mu,\sigma,\lambda)$, with independent bounded-uniform priors: $N_s,N_b\in(0,5N)$ with $N=17839$ the number of selected events, $A\in(-1,1)$, $\mu\in(5260,5310)\,\MeV$, $\sigma\in(3,40)\,\MeV$, $\lambda\in(10^{-5},0.05)\,\MeV^{-1}$. Bounds and the walker starting point were chosen from the preselected mass spectrum (peak at $\mu\approx5283\,\MeV$).

Given the data $D=\{(m_i,q_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ (mass and charge of each candidate), Bayes' theorem gives the posterior
\begin{equation}
p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{p(D)},
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
with the extended likelihood of the two charge samples
\begin{multline}
\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta)\propto e^{-(N_s+N_b)}
\prod_{i:\,q_i=+1}\Big[N_s^{+}S(m_i)+\tfrac{N_b}{2}B(m_i)\Big] \\
\times\prod_{i:\,q_i=-1}\Big[N_s^{-}S(m_i)+\tfrac{N_b}{2}B(m_i)\Big],
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{multline}
where $S(m_i)\equiv S(m_i;\mu,\sigma)$, $B(m_i)\equiv B(m_i;\lambda)$, and $N_s^\pm=N_s(1\mp A)/2$. The prior factorizes into the uniform densities above,
\begin{equation}
\pi(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{6}\frac{\mathbf{1}\left[\theta_k\in(a_k,b_k)\right]}{b_k-a_k},
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
so inside the bounds the posterior is proportional to the likelihood. The evidence $p(D)$ is a constant normalization: the MCMC samples $\mathcal{L}\,\pi$ without computing it, and the Savage-Dickey ratio of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} avoids computing it as well. The marginal posterior of the asymmetry is obtained by integrating out the five nuisance parameters, $p(A\mid D)=\int p(\theta\mid D)\,dN_s\,dN_b\,d\mu\,d\sigma\,d\lambda$; with MCMC this amounts to taking the histogram of the $A$ component of the chain.

\subsection{MCMC Sampling and Convergence Diagnostics}
\label{sec:bayes_mcmc}
The posterior was sampled with \texttt{emcee} using an ensemble of 32 walkers initialized in a narrow ball around the starting point, run for 9000 steps with the first 1500 ($\sim$1/6) discarded as burn-in, leaving $2.4\times10^5$ samples. The mean acceptance fraction is $0.52$. Convergence was checked through the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau$ of each parameter, $\tau\in[58,79]$ steps: the post-burn-in chain is $N/\tau\geq95$ autocorrelation times long for every parameter, above the usual threshold of $50$ for a reliable $\tau$ estimate, and corresponds to $\gtrsim3000$ effectively independent samples per parameter.

\subsection{Posterior Results and Credible Intervals}
\label{sec:bayes_posterior}
The marginal posterior on the raw asymmetry gives (median and central intervals)
\begin{gather*}
\Araw = -0.0497, \\
68\%~\text{CI}~[-0.058,-0.041], \qquad 95\%~\text{CI}~[-0.066,-0.033].
\end{gather*}
The posterior is very close to Gaussian, with standard deviation $0.0083$. The result has the same sign and order of magnitude as the published $\ACP(KKK)=-0.037\pm0.002\,(\text{stat})\pm0.002\,(\text{syst})\pm0.003\,(J/\psi K)$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023,lhcb_cpv_paper}; this is a sanity check only, since $\Araw$ is not corrected for detection or production asymmetries and is therefore not directly comparable to $\ACP$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}). The fitted yields are $N_s=15281\pm130$ and $N_b=2555\pm65$, with $\mu=5284.41\pm0.16\,\MeV$ and $\sigma=17.95\pm0.13\,\MeV$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_corner}).

\subsection{Bayes Factor: $A=0$ vs $A$ Free}
\label{sec:bayes_factor}
Computing the evidence of each hypothesis by direct numerical integration is impractical for a 6-parameter posterior. Since $H_0$ ($A=0$) is nested in $H_1$ ($A$ free) and the priors on the other parameters are shared, the Bayes factor reduces to the Savage-Dickey density ratio~\cite{dickey1971},
\[
BF_{01} = \frac{p(A=0\mid\text{data})}{\pi(A=0)}, \qquad \pi(A=0)=\tfrac12 .
\]
$A=0$ lies $6.0$ posterior standard deviations from the median, deep in a tail where the chain contains essentially no samples. Estimating $p(A=0\mid\text{data})$ with a Gaussian KDE on the $A$ chain is therefore unreliable: at the Silverman bandwidth it gives $BF_{01}\approx3\times10^{-59}$, and rescaling the bandwidth by $0.5$--$3$ moves the result between $10^{-228}$ and $10^{-9}$, since the KDE value at $A=0$ only reflects the kernel tails of the few most extreme samples. Because the marginal posterior of $A$ is very close to Gaussian, a more meaningful estimate uses its Gaussian approximation, giving $BF_{01}\approx1.3\times10^{-6}$ ($\ln BF_{10}\approx13.5$): decisive evidence against $A=0$ on the Jeffreys scale, as is every KDE value above. As for any Bayes factor of a point null against a free parameter, the value scales with the prior width (Lindley's paradox): with the narrower prior $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}, $\pi(A=0)$ grows to $1/0.6$ and $BF_{01}$ drops by a factor $3.3$, which leaves the conclusion unchanged.

\subsection{Goodness-of-Fit and Signal Model Comparison}
\label{sec:bayes_gof}
A binned comparison of data and posterior-median model ($\chi^2=\sum(\text{obs}-\text{exp})^2/\text{exp}$ over 30 mass bins per charge) shows that the minimal Gaussian signal model does not fully describe the data: $\chi^2/\text{ndof}=127.4/54=2.36$, with $\chi^2=80.4$ for $B^+$ and $47.1$ for $B^-$. The largest pulls sit on the two flanks of the peak, about $\pm3\sigma$ from $\mu$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_pulls}): the observed signal shape has heavier tails than a Gaussian. Table~\ref{tab:gof} compares three signal shapes. A Crystal Ball with tail parameters fixed to typical values ($\beta=1.5$, $m=3$) makes the fit worse, not better. Letting $\beta$ and $m$ float (an 8-parameter fit) improves $\chi^2/\text{ndof}$ to $2.00$, with $\beta=1.83$ and $m=12.4$, i.e.\ a tail specific to this dataset. Across the three models $\Araw$ changes by at most $0.0015$, a fifth of its statistical uncertainty, so the asymmetry does not depend on the signal shape even though none of the models reaches $\chi^2/\text{ndof}\approx1$.

\subsection{Robustness Checks: Mass Window, Background Model, Prior, Magnet Polarity}
\label{sec:bayes_syst}
The nominal fit was repeated under three alternative modeling choices -- a narrower window ($[5200,5450]\,\MeV$), a window without the sparse high-mass tail ($[5150,5400]\,\MeV$), and a linear instead of exponential background -- and with a narrower prior on the asymmetry, $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$, as a prior-sensitivity check. All four use the nominal chain length (9000 steps, 32 walkers). They give $\Araw\in[-0.0498,-0.0495]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_syst_forest}, Table~\ref{tab:bayes_systematics}): the spread, $3\times10^{-4}$, is about 4\% of the statistical uncertainty and only about twice the Monte Carlo error on the posterior median ($\sim$$1.4\times10^{-4}$), so no sensitivity to these choices is detected.

As an experimental cross-check, the fit was repeated separately on the two magnet polarities, whose left-right detection asymmetries are opposite and partially cancel in the combined sample: $\Araw(\text{down})=-0.055$ ($[-0.065,-0.044]$, $n=10500$) and $\Araw(\text{up})=-0.043$ ($[-0.056,-0.030]$, $n=7339$). They differ by $0.7\sigma$, so there is no indication that the asymmetry is an artifact of one polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_magnet}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/bayes_magnet_posterior.pdf}
\caption{Posterior on $\Araw$ for the magnet-down-only, magnet-up-only, and combined fits. The two single-polarity posteriors differ by $0.7\sigma$.}
\label{fig:bayes_magnet}

The assumption of a charge-symmetric background, $N_b^{\pm}=N_b/2$, was tested on the sidebands ($|m-\mu|>4\sigma$, where the expected signal contamination is negligible): their counting asymmetry is $A_{\text{sideband}}=-0.047\pm0.029$ ($n=1217$), $1.6\sigma$ from zero and equally compatible with the signal asymmetry. The same picture emerges from the full fit-window count (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_exploration}), which is closer to $\Araw$ than a symmetric background would allow. The check is therefore inconclusive rather than reassuring. The definitive test is a fit with independent background yields $N_b^+$, $N_b^-$, as in the frequentist joint fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}). A simple estimate suggests the impact on $\Araw$ is small: if the background carried $A_b=A_{\text{sideband}}$, attributing the excess $A_b N_b$ to the background rather than to the signal would give $(N^--N^+-A_bN_b)/N_s\approx-0.0495$.

\subsection{Toy-MC Coverage Validation}
\label{sec:bayes_toymc}
To check whether the credible intervals have the claimed frequentist coverage, 500 pseudo-datasets of the same size as the data were generated from the fitted model (parametric bootstrap, with the posterior-median $\theta$ as ground truth) and each was refit with a 3500-step chain. The fraction of replicas whose interval contains the injected $A$ is $0.664$ for the 68\% CI ($0.8\sigma$ below nominal, binomial uncertainty $0.021$) and $0.962$ for the 95\% CI ($1.2\sigma$ above nominal): the intervals have correct coverage. This is a closure test of the inference procedure: since the toys are generated from the same model that is fitted, it does not probe the model mismatch of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}, which is instead addressed by the signal-model and robustness checks.

\subsection{Local Dalitz-Plot Asymmetry (Fixed Binning)}
\label{sec:bayes_dalitz}
To test whether the asymmetry is uniform over phase space or concentrated in specific regions, each candidate was placed in the Dalitz plane using the two opposite-sign $K^+K^-$ invariant masses $m^2(H1,H2)$ and $m^2(H1,H3)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_presel}), sorted into $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ and $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ to remove the $H2/H3$ labeling ambiguity. The plane was divided into an $8\times8$ grid. In each bin with at least 20 candidates (29 of 64), the local asymmetry $A_i=(N_i^--N_i^+)/N_i$, with binomial uncertainty $\sigma(A_i)=\sqrt{(1-A_i^2)/N_i}$, was compared with the global counting asymmetry $A_{\mathrm{glob}}=-0.049$ through $S_i=(A_i-A_{\mathrm{glob}})/\sigma(A_i)$.

Three of the 29 bins have $|S_i|>3$, all with $A_i$ more negative than the global value; about $0.08$ would be expected if the asymmetry were uniform, with a probability of $7\times10^{-5}$ for three or more. A global test of homogeneity, $\chi^2=\sum_i S_i^2=85.6$ for 28 degrees of freedom ($p\approx10^{-7}$), confirms that the local asymmetry is not uniform. The most negative bins lie at low $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_dalitz}). Testing against zero instead of $A_{\mathrm{glob}}$ would partly reflect the global asymmetry itself. Restricting the sample to the signal window ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $n=15{,}119$, $\approx$96\% signal purity from the fitted model) gives the same picture -- 29 populated bins, three with $|S_i|>3$, $\chi^2=95.5/28$ -- so the structure is not produced by the combinatorial background. Two caveats apply: the binning is coarse and was not optimized, and $\sigma(A_i)$ ignores the background fluctuations, which a proper sWeight-based background subtraction would include (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Section~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans} revisits the same question with an adaptive, data-driven binning ($K$-means).

\section{Machine Learning Module}
\label{sec:ml}
As a complementary, purely data-driven approach to the signal/background separation used in the mass fits (Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:bayesian}), a supervised classifier was trained on weak labels derived from the mass sidebands and applied to study whether an ML-assisted event selection biases $\Araw$, and whether it can resolve local Dalitz-plot structure that a fixed binning would miss.

\subsection{Weak Labels and Feature Set}
\label{sec:ml_labels}
Weak labels were built from the same mass model as the Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_model}, $\mu=5284.4$, $\sigma=17.95\,\MeV$), without ever using the reconstructed $B$ mass itself as an input feature, to avoid mass sculpting. Events with $|m-\mu|<2\sigma$ (i.e.\ $m\in[5248.5,5320.3]\,\MeV$) are labeled signal-like; events with $|m-\mu|>4\sigma$ (outside $[5212.6,5356.2]\,\MeV$, within the fit window) are labeled background-like; the intermediate $2$--$4\sigma$ gap is left unlabeled and excluded, to keep the two classes cleanly separated. This gives $n_{\mathrm{labeled}}=16336$ events, of which $15119$ are signal-like and only $1217$ background-like (imbalance ratio $0.080$) -- a severe class imbalance, handled downstream with \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'} for the scikit-learn models, a sample-weight approximation for GBM (which has no native \texttt{class\_weight} support), and explicit class weights in the Keras loss for the FFNN.

The feature set consists of the 6 per-track kinematic/PID variables (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}, \texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}, \texttt{IPChi2}) for each of the 3 final-state tracks (18 features), plus two $B$-candidate variables not used in the preselection: \texttt{B\_FlightDistance} and \texttt{B\_VertexChi2}, both standard LHCb combinatorial-background discriminants (a genuine $B$ flies further before decaying and has a better-reconstructed vertex than a random track combination). Both separate the weak-label classes significantly (Kolmogorov-Smirnov $p<10^{-35}$ for each) and are not redundant with the per-track features (Spearman $|\rho|<0.52$ with each of the 18), giving $20$ features in total. An exploratory PCA on the standardized features shows $15$ of $20$ components are needed to reach $90\%$ explained variance -- the features carry mostly independent information, so no dimensionality reduction is applied to the models used downstream (PCA is kept as a diagnostic only). The labeled sample was split $60/20/20$ into train/validation/test, stratified on the label, with the scaler fit on the training set only.

\label{sec:ml_models}

Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} summarizes 5-fold cross-validated AUC on the training set (not computed for the FFNN, evaluated on the held-out validation set instead) and AUC on the untouched test set. \subsection{Regularization, Bias-Variance, and Calibration}
\label{sec:ml_regcal}
An $L1$/$L2$ regularization sweep on the logistic regression (log-spaced grid on $C=1/\alpha$, with CV) finds essentially identical optimal AUC for both penalties ($C_{L1}=0.024$, AUC $=0.7280$; $C_{L2}=45.2$, AUC $=0.7273$), with 3 of the 20 features driven to exactly zero by the $L1$ penalty: the linear model does not overfit and gains little from regularization, consistent with its comparatively low AUC in Table~\ref{tab:ml_models}.

A bias-variance complexity curve was built for GBM using \texttt{staged\_predict} (train/test AUC as a function of the number of boosting stages). The train/test AUC gap grows from $0.014$ at $20$ trees to $0.033$ at the final iteration -- mild but measurable overfitting as complexity increases, though the gap stays small relative to the overall AUC scale ($\sim$0.81).

Calibration was assessed via the Brier score for all six models (Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} companion metric): Logistic Regression $0.208$, Logistic Regression (L1) $0.212$, Decision Tree $0.153$, Random Forest $0.132$, Gradient Boosting $0.125$, FFNN $0.071$. Notably the FFNN, despite the lowest AUC of the four non-linear models, has by far the best-calibrated raw score -- a model can rank events well (high AUC) without its output being a trustworthy probability, and vice versa. Applying isotonic recalibration (\texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV}) to the reference Random Forest model reduces its Brier score from $0.132$ to $0.053$ (a $60\%$ reduction): the RF score is a good \emph{ranking} but, uncalibrated, a poor \emph{probability} estimate.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:ml_uncertainty}
Two independent estimates of epistemic (model) uncertainty were computed per event: MC Dropout on the FFNN (dropout kept active at inference, $300$ repeated forward passes, uncertainty = std across passes) and ensemble variance on the Random Forest (uncertainty = std of the individual trees' predictions). Since the FFNN result depends on the training, the MC-dropout figures below are the mean and standard deviation over 10 retrainings with different random seeds. The mean per-event uncertainty is $0.0126\pm0.0023$ for MC dropout (range $0.0098$--$0.0166$) and $0.185$ for the RF ensemble, a factor $15\pm2.5$ (range $11$--$19$) apart in absolute scale.

Two checks were run to understand the discrepancy. First, whether the class imbalance (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}) inflates RF tree-to-tree variance on the rare background-like class: the mean RF std is $0.196$ on background-like test events vs.\ $0.185$ on signal-like -- only a $\sim$6\% relative difference, too small to explain the gap. The same comparison for MC dropout shows a much larger relative effect ($0.024\pm0.003$ background vs.\ $0.012\pm0.002$ signal, a factor $\sim$2.1 in every retraining), suggesting the FFNN's epistemic uncertainty is more sensitive to the scarcity of background-like training examples than the RF's is. Second, whether the two methods at least agree on \emph{which} events are most uncertain, independent of absolute scale: the Spearman correlation between per-event MC-dropout and RF-ensemble uncertainty is $\rho=0.36\pm0.02$ ($p<10^{-70}$ in every retraining) -- highly significant but moderate, meaning the two estimates partly agree on which events are most uncertain, but are far from interchangeable.

\subsection{K-means on the Dalitz Plot}
\label{sec:ml_kmeans}
As an adaptive alternative to the fixed Dalitz-plot binning, $K$-means clustering ($K=8$, matching the fixed-binning granularity for direct comparison) was run on the standardized kinematic features. Repeating the clustering with different random seeds gives an adjusted Rand index of $0.996$ between runs, indicating a highly stable partition. Cluster sizes range from $n=1216$ to $n=4007$, and the local raw asymmetry $A_{\mathrm{local}}$ per cluster ranges from $-0.139$ to $+0.035$. Of the 8 clusters, exactly one shows a local deviation from zero exceeding $3\sigma$: cluster 2 ($n=3341$, $A_{\mathrm{local}}=-0.139$). Restricting that cluster to the high-purity signal window ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $n_{\mathrm{purified}}=2880$, $\sim$85\% purity) gives $A_{\mathrm{local}}=-0.147$ -- the asymmetry survives, and if anything strengthens slightly, after purification, disfavoring background contamination as its origin.

$K=8$ was chosen for comparability with the fixed binning rather than from a clustering-quality criterion; the elbow (inertia) and silhouette scores over $K=2$--$12$ were inspected as a check. The global silhouette optimum is $K=3$ ($0.467$), but $K=8$ sits at a local silhouette maximum ($0.410$, higher than the neighboring $K=6,7$ and $K=9$--$12$), so it is not an arbitrary choice, just not the statistically optimal one -- a coarser, $K=3$ partition would likely be less directly comparable to the $8\times8$ fixed binning of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}.

\subsection{Stability of $A_{\mathrm{raw}}$ under ML Selection}
\label{sec:ml_stability}
The reference Random Forest score was applied to \emph{all} events in the fit window (not only the labeled subsample used for training), and the counting asymmetry $\Araw$ was recomputed after cutting at score thresholds from $0.1$ to $0.9$, with non-parametric bootstrap uncertainties (resampling the full selected sample and repeating cut+count) at each threshold. Across thresholds $0.1$--$0.7$, $\Araw$ stays in a narrow band, $[-0.054,-0.049]$, consistent within uncertainty with the nominal Bayesian fit result ($-0.0497$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}) and with bootstrap errors that are small and stable ($0.007$--$0.010$). At threshold $0.8$, $\Araw$ drifts to $-0.014$, but the bootstrap uncertainty there is also much larger ($0.026$), so the drift is only $\sim$1.4$\sigma$ from nominal -- suggestive but not significant. At threshold $0.9$ the selection becomes fully degenerate ($\Araw=-1$, every surviving event has the same charge, bootstrap uncertainty undefined) -- too few events survive to draw any statistical conclusion. Over the range where the selection retains a usable sample size, the ML-based event selection does not introduce a detectable bias on $\Araw$.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/ml_threshold_stability.pdf}
\caption{$\Araw\pm$bootstrap uncertainty vs.\ RF-score threshold, against the nominal Bayesian fit result (dotted line, shaded 68\% CI). Stable through threshold $0.7$; at threshold $0.9$ only 2 events survive and $\Araw=-1$ (marked off scale).}
\label{fig:ml_threshold}
\end{figure}

Since the threshold sweep is post-cut counting, it does not test whether the ML selection biases a \emph{fit}-based estimate. To test this, the Bayesian mass model (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_model}) was refit -- same parametrization, same MCMC settings (32 walkers, 9000 steps) -- on the RF-selected subsample alone (score $>0.5$, $n=15204$), instead of counting. The result, $\Araw=-0.0493$ (68\% CI $[-0.058,-0.041]$), is consistent with both the nominal full-sample fit ($-0.0497$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}) and the simple-counting estimate on the same subsample ($-0.0499$) -- the ML selection does not bias the mass-fit estimate either. \todo{frequentist refit on the same ML-selected sample, once Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist} is final.}

As with the Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}), the ML-selected sample was also cross-checked by magnet polarity, to test whether the RF cut interacts with the detector's left-right instrumental asymmetry (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}) rather than tracking a genuine physical effect. At the representative RF-score threshold of $0.5$: $\Araw(\text{down})=-0.062\pm0.011$ ($n=8871$) and $\Araw(\text{up})=-0.032\pm0.013$ ($n=6333$). The two are individually somewhat more separated than in the Bayesian check ($\sim$1.8$\sigma$ apart here, vs.\ fully overlapping there), but follow the same sign pattern (down more negative than up) and their 68\% intervals still overlap -- consistent with the same underlying physical asymmetry seen through a smaller, ML-selected sample, not with a polarity-dependent artifact of the classifier.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several modeling choices and known weaknesses limit how far the results of Secs.~\ref{sec:bayesian}--\ref{sec:ml} can be pushed, and are collected here rather than scattered through the results sections.

On the Bayesian side (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayesian}): the background is assumed to split equally between $B$ charges, while the sideband asymmetry ($-0.047\pm0.029$) and the full-window count both point to a background asymmetry of similar size to the signal one (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}) -- the estimated impact on $\Araw$ is small, but a fit with independent $N_b^\pm$ is needed to settle it; the Savage-Dickey Bayes factor of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} is known only approximately ($BF_{01}\sim10^{-6}$ from the Gaussian approximation, KDE estimates unusable this far in the tail) and depends on the prior width, though the conclusion (decisive evidence against $A=0$) is robust; the toy-MC coverage check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_toymc} is a closure test and cannot detect model misspecification; even the best-fitting signal model (Crystal Ball with floated tail parameters) leaves $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}\approx2$, not $\approx1$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}); and the fixed-binning Dalitz analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz} uses a coarse $8\times8$ grid and a simple mass-window cut rather than a proper background subtraction.

On the machine-learning side (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml}): the weak labels suffer from severe class imbalance ($1217$ background-like vs.\ $15{,}119$ signal-like events, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}); the two epistemic-uncertainty estimators (MC Dropout, RF ensemble-variance) disagree by a factor $\sim$15 in absolute scale and are only moderately correlated event-by-event ($\rho\approx0.36$, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_uncertainty}), and the MC-dropout estimate itself varies by $\sim$20\% between trainings; the test set is small ($0.017$ uncertainty on each test AUC), so the hyperparameter searches (Tables~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams}--\ref{tab:ml_ffnn_search}) cannot resolve test-AUC differences of the size observed; and the stability of $\Araw$ under the ML selection was checked with a Bayesian refit only (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_stability}).

More generally, no detector-level systematic correction (selection efficiency, production asymmetry) is applied anywhere in this work, so $\Araw$ as reported throughout is explicitly \emph{not} a measurement of $\ACP$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}).

The most natural next steps, in rough priority order, are: replacing the fixed-cut local Dalitz asymmetries (both binned, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}, and $K$-means, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans}) with a proper sWeight-based background subtraction; fitting the background yields of the two charges independently; and applying the frequentist fit to the ML-selected sample.

For GBM and the FFNN (Tables~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams} and \ref{tab:ml_ffnn_search}), the configuration with the best \emph{cross-validated} score has a slightly \emph{lower} test AUC than the untuned default ($-0.006$ for GBM, $-0.001$ for the FFNN), while for Random Forest both improve ($+0.009$ on test). The sign of the GBM and FFNN differences is what the ``winner's curse'' of hyperparameter selection predicts: taking the arg-max over many (100--170) noisy cross-validation estimates overestimates the selected configuration's generalization performance, because the selection favors configurations with a favorable CV fluctuation, and widening the grid enlarges this bias. None of the test-AUC differences is significant, however: all are well below the $0.017$ statistical uncertainty of each test AUC, and for the FFNN also below the $0.004$ spread between retrainings of the same configuration. The searches therefore show that no tested configuration improves significantly on the defaults; the CV scores of the selected configurations should be read as optimistic estimates of their performance.
