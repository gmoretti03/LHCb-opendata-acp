\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Charge-parity (CP) violation -- the small observed asymmetry between the behavior of matter and antimatter -- is one of the necessary ingredients for the matter-dominated universe we observe, yet the CP violation built into the Standard Model is far too small to account for it. Studying CP violation in the decays of $B$ mesons, where it is comparatively large and experimentally accessible, is one of the main ways to test the Standard Model's CKM description of the effect and search for physics beyond it. Charmless three-body decays such as $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ ($h=K,\pi$) are especially interesting in this respect: unlike a two-body decay, the three-body phase space (the Dalitz plot) allows the raw asymmetry $\Araw$ to be studied not only as a single global number, but also \emph{locally}, since interference between intermediate resonances can concentrate CP-violating effects in specific kinematic regions rather than spreading them uniformly~\cite{lhcb_cpv_paper}.

This project uses the LHCb Open Data record for $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$~\cite{opendata_record} to address the research question: \emph{to what extent do frequentist likelihood fits, Bayesian inference, and machine-learning-assisted event selection produce consistent, well-controlled estimates of the raw asymmetry $\Araw$ in this decay?} The goal is not to reproduce a competitive LHCb-quality measurement, but to build, on top of a validated public dataset and starting notebook~\cite{opendata_notebook}, a methodologically rigorous project that puts these three inference frameworks -- covered separately in the course -- side by side on the exact same data and the exact same physical question. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset and the preselection shared by all three modules; Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:ml} present the frequentist, Bayesian, and machine-learning analyses respectively; Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} brings the three together.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset and Preselection}
\label{sec:data_presel}
The dataset is the CERN Open Data record 4900, ``Matter Antimatter Differences -- B meson decays to three hadrons''~\cite{opendata_record}: candidates for $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ decays collected by LHCb in 2011 at $\sqrt{s}=7$\,TeV, reconstructed with the LHCb software and already passed through trigger and offline selection. The data are distributed as ROOT \texttt{TTree}s (\texttt{DecayTree}) containing only the most relevant reconstructed branches, split across two files by magnet polarity (magnet up/down), for a combined $8{,}556{,}118$ candidates. The same record also includes a simplified, detector-response-free phase-space simulation of $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$; it is not used anywhere in this project (no multi-channel phase-space generation was pursued, to keep the project in scope) and is mentioned here only for completeness.

For each of the three final-state tracks ($H1$, $H2$, $H3$) the analysis reads momentum components (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}), PID likelihoods (\texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}), a muon flag (\texttt{isMuon}), and the track charge -- 18 kinematic/PID branches plus 3 charges. All three analysis modules (frequentist, Bayesian, machine learning) share the same preselection, agreed at the start of the project so results stay comparable: a kaon-PID cut and a muon veto applied to all three tracks,
\[
\texttt{H*\_ProbPi} < 0.5 \ \wedge\ \texttt{H*\_ProbK} > 0.5 \ \wedge\ \lnot\texttt{H*\_isMuon},
\]
which retains $23{,}895$ of the $8{,}556{,}118$ read candidates ($0.28\%$) -- the PID requirement, applied on all three tracks simultaneously, is by far the dominant reduction, consistent with selecting a comparatively pure $KKK$ final state out of a much larger, PID-unconstrained sample.

While implementing the Dalitz-plot analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans}), it was found that in this dataset $H1$ is, by construction, always the track of opposite charge to the other two: only the charge combinations $(+1,-1,-1)$ and $(-1,+1,+1)$ occur for $(H1,H2,H3)$. Equivalently, $H2$ and $H3$ are always the same-sign, interchangeable pair (the two kaons sharing the sign of the $B$), while $H1$ is the unique ``bachelor'' track of the opposite sign -- a labeling convention that matters when constructing unambiguous Dalitz-plot variables from the two same-sign-pair invariant masses.

\subsection{Invariant Mass Reconstruction}
\label{sec:data_mass}
The three final-state tracks are assigned the charged-kaon mass hypothesis ($M_K=493.677\,\MeV$) regardless of their PID likelihoods, and their four-momenta are summed to reconstruct the $B$ candidate, $m(KKK)=\sqrt{(\sum_i E_i)^2-|\sum_i \vec{p}_i|^2}$. A first look at the full reconstructed mass range showed a rise in combinatorial background below $\sim$5150\,\MeV, consistent with a partially-reconstructed contribution that a single exponential background shape cannot describe; the fit window used throughout this project -- by both the frequentist and Bayesian modules, and to build the ML weak labels (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}) -- was therefore set to $[5150,5450]\,\MeV$. This leaves $17{,}839$ preselected candidates in the fit window: $9358$ with $B$-charge $+1$ and $8481$ with $B$-charge $-1$, split $10{,}500$ (magnet down) / $7339$ (magnet up).

\subsection{Data Exploration}
\label{sec:data_exploration}
Figure~\ref{fig:mass_spectrum} shows the reconstructed mass spectrum in the fit window, split by $B$ charge (left) and by magnet polarity (right). The spectrum peaks at $m\approx5284\,\MeV$ with a visible signal excess over a falling combinatorial background, and its shape is qualitatively similar for the two charges and the two magnet polarities -- as expected, since a large raw asymmetry would already be visible by eye at this level. The simple charge-counting asymmetry on the full fit-window sample, $A_{\mathrm{count}}=(N^--N^+)/(N^-+N^+)=-0.049$, is consistent with every fit-based estimate obtained later in the project (Secs.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}, \ref{sec:ml_stability}) and serves as a useful, assumption-free sanity check throughout. For the ML module, the corresponding weak-label class balance ($15{,}119$ signal-like vs.\ $1217$ background-like events, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}) reflects the same high-purity signal region, seen here from the sideband side rather than the counting side.

\label{sec:freq_ci}

\[

\section{Bayesian Analysis}
\label{sec:bayesian}

\subsection{Model and Priors}
\label{sec:bayes_model}
The Bayesian analysis reuses the same physical mass model as the frequentist fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}): a signal-plus-background mixture
\[
f(m) = N_s\, S(m;\mu,\sigma) + N_b\, B(m;\lambda),
\]
with $S$ a truncated Gaussian and $B$ a truncated exponential, both normalized on the fit window $[5150,5450]\,\MeV$. The window was narrowed from the full simulation range to avoid a rise in background below $5150\,\MeV$, consistent with a partially-reconstructed contribution that a single exponential cannot describe (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}). The signal yield is split between the two $B$-meson charges through a simultaneous parametrization, $N_s^+ = N_s(1-A)/2$ and $N_s^- = N_s(1+A)/2$, so that $A$ is fit directly rather than derived from two independent yields; the background is assumed to be shared equally between charges, $N_b^{\pm}=N_b/2$.

The full parameter vector is $\theta=(N_s,N_b,A,\mu,\sigma,\lambda)$, with independent bounded-uniform priors (returning a flat log-density inside the bound and $-\infty$ outside), following the convention used throughout the course's own MCMC exercises: $N_s,N_b\in(0,5N)$ with $N=17839$ the number of selected events, $A\in(-1,1)$, $\mu\in(5260,5310)\,\MeV$, $\sigma\in(3,40)\,\MeV$, $\lambda\in(10^{-5},0.05)\,\MeV^{-1}$. Bounds and the walker starting point were set from a first look at the real preselected mass spectrum (peak at $\mu\approx5283\,\MeV$).

\subsection{MCMC Sampling and Convergence Diagnostics}
\label{sec:bayes_mcmc}
The posterior was sampled with \texttt{emcee} using an ensemble of 32 walkers initialized in a narrow ball around the data-driven starting point, run for 9000 steps with the first 1500 ($\sim$1/6) discarded as burn-in. The mean acceptance fraction was $0.52$. Convergence was checked via the integrated autocorrelation time $\tau$ for each parameter ($\tau\in[58,79]$ steps), giving $N/\tau\gtrsim110$ post-burn-in samples per walker for every parameter -- comfortably above the rule-of-thumb threshold of 50 recommended for a reliable $\tau$ estimate. This diagnostic is not part of the course material (no exercise notebook computes it) and was added as a deliberate methodological addition, since neither posterior summaries nor credible intervals are trustworthy without first checking the chains have mixed.

\subsection{Posterior Results and Credible Intervals}
\label{sec:bayes_posterior}
The marginal posterior on the raw asymmetry is
\[
\Araw = -0.0497,
\]
with $68\%$ CI $[-0.058,-0.041]$ and $95\%$ CI $[-0.066,-0.033]$,
consistent with the naive counting asymmetry on the same sample ($-0.049$) and with the same sign and order of magnitude as the published $\ACP(KKK)=-0.037\pm0.003$ from Ref.~\cite{lhcb_cpv_paper} -- a sanity check only, since $\Araw$ is not corrected for selection efficiency or production asymmetry and is therefore not directly comparable to $\ACP$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection{Bayes Factor: $A=0$ vs $A$ Free}
\label{sec:bayes_factor}
The course's own worked example (Week 4) computes a Bayes factor by direct numerical integration of the evidence on a 1D grid, which does not scale to our 6-parameter posterior. We instead use the Savage-Dickey density ratio,
\[
BF_{01} = \frac{p(A=0\mid\text{data})}{\pi(A=0)},
\]
estimating the posterior density at $A=0$ with a Gaussian KDE on the flattened $A$ chain and using the analytically known uniform prior density $\pi(A=0)=1/2$; equal prior odds between $H_0$ ($A=0$) and $H_1$ ($A$ free) are assumed, so $BF_{01}$ equals the posterior odds directly. At the default (Silverman) bandwidth, $BF_{01}\approx3\times10^{-59}$, overwhelming evidence against $A=0$. However, $BF_{01}$ is extremely sensitive to the KDE bandwidth -- varying it by a factor 0.5--3 changes the estimate by more than 200 orders of magnitude ($2.5\times10^{-228}$ to $1.7\times10^{-9}$), because $A=0$ lies roughly $6\sigma$ into the tail of a posterior that is many bandwidths wide. The exact value of $BF_{01}$ is therefore not meaningful, but the qualitative conclusion -- decisive evidence against $A=0$ -- is robust across the entire tested bandwidth range.

\subsection{Goodness-of-Fit and Signal Model Comparison}
\label{sec:bayes_gof}
A binned pull comparison ($\chi^2=\sum(\text{obs}-\text{exp})^2/\text{exp}$ over 30 mass bins per charge) shows the minimal Gaussian signal model does not fully describe the data: $\chi^2/\text{ndof}=2.36$ (54 dof). Table~\ref{tab:gof} compares three signal-shape choices. Fixing the Crystal Ball tail parameters to literature-typical values ($\beta=1.5$, $m=3$) makes the fit \emph{worse}, not better -- the fixed tail shape does not match this dataset. Letting $\beta,m$ float (an 8-parameter fit) improves $\chi^2/\text{ndof}$ to $2.00$, converging to $\beta=1.83$, $m=12.4$ -- far from the literature guess, indicating a dataset-specific tail rather than a universal one. Across all three signal models $\Araw$ stays consistent within uncertainties, so the physics conclusion does not depend on the choice of signal shape, even though none of the three models reaches $\chi^2/\text{ndof}\approx1$.

\subsection{Systematic Checks: Mass Window, Background Model, Magnet Polarity}
\label{sec:bayes_syst}
Four variations of the nominal fit were repeated to probe sensitivity to modeling choices: a narrower window ($[5200,5450]\,\MeV$), a window without the sparse high-mass tail ($[5150,5400]\,\MeV$), a linear background shape instead of the exponential, and a narrower prior on $A$ ($[-0.3,0.3]$). Initially run with shorter (2500-step) chains for speed, then rerun with the full nominal chain length (9000 steps, 32 walkers) for a definitive check: all four give $\Araw\in[-0.0498,-0.0495]$ -- if anything an even tighter spread than the short-chain pass, differences smaller than the fourth decimal digit and well inside the statistical uncertainty ($\pm0.008$) -- so the result is robust to every modeling choice tested, confirmed with full-length chains (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_syst_forest}).

The two credible intervals overlap substantially, giving no indication that the measured asymmetry is an artifact of a single magnet polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_magnet}).

The model also assumes the background splits evenly between charges, $N_b^{\pm}=N_b/2$ -- an assumption so far only reassured indirectly by the magnet cross-check above. This was tested directly using the sidebands ($|m-\mu|>4\sigma$, background-dominated by construction): the simple counting asymmetry there is $A_{\text{sideband}}=-0.047\pm0.029$ ($n=1217$), compatible with zero at $1.6\sigma$ -- not significant, but with a sample this small (and a point estimate close in sign and magnitude to the signal-region $\Araw$ itself) the check has limited power and does not fully resolve the assumption, only adds a genuine empirical data point in its favor.

\subsection{Toy-MC Coverage Validation}
\label{sec:bayes_toymc}
To check whether the reported credible intervals actually have the claimed frequentist coverage, pseudo-datasets of the same size as the real data were generated directly from the fitted model (parametric bootstrap, injecting the observed posterior-median $\theta$ as ground truth), each refit with a shorter (3500-step) chain, and the fraction of replicas whose 68\%/95\% credible interval contains the injected $A$ was recorded. An initial pass with 60 replicas (3000-step chains) gave an empirical 68\% coverage of $0.60$ against a nominal $0.68$ ($\sim$1.3$\sigma$ hint of mild undercoverage). Progressively rerun with more replicas for tighter statistics -- 100 replicas gave $0.63$ ($\sim$1.1$\sigma$); the final pass with 500 replicas gives an empirical 68\% coverage of $0.664$, only $\sim$0.8$\sigma$ from nominal, and a 95\% coverage of $0.962$ against nominal $0.95$. The mild undercoverage hint from the first (60-replica) pass does not survive the larger replica count and is consistent with a statistical fluctuation.

\subsection{Local Dalitz-Plot Asymmetry (Fixed Binning)}
\label{sec:bayes_dalitz}
As a first, fixed-binning look at whether the raw asymmetry is concentrated in specific regions of phase space (as opposed to the single global $\Araw$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}), the two same-sign-pair invariant masses $m^2(H1,H2)$ and $m^2(H1,H3)$ were computed using the $H1$-is-bachelor convention (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_presel}) and sorted into $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$/$m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ to remove the $H2/H3$ labeling ambiguity, following the same approach as the official starting notebook. The Dalitz plane was divided into an $8\times8$ grid; in each bin with at least 20 events the local asymmetry $A_i=(N_i^--N_i^+)/N_i^{\mathrm{tot}}$ and its binomial-type uncertainty $\sigma(A_i)=\sqrt{(1-A_i^2)/N_i^{\mathrm{tot}}}$ were computed, giving a local significance $S_i=A_i/\sigma(A_i)$.

Using all events in the fit window, 29 of the 64 bins are populated above the 20-event threshold, and $4$ of those $29$ show $|S_i|>3$ -- a hint of localized structure beyond the single global asymmetry, though with an $8\times8$ binning this is only a coarse, exploratory look (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Repeating the same binning and threshold on the signal-window-only subsample ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $n=15{,}119$, $>$95\% signal purity by sideband counting) gives an identical result -- $29$ populated bins, $4$ significant at $3\sigma$ -- so this local structure is not an artifact of combinatorial background diluting the fit-window sample, though a fixed cut is not a substitute for a proper sideband/sWeight-based background subtraction (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}). Section~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans} revisits the same question with an adaptive, data-driven binning ($K$-means) instead of this fixed grid.

\section{Machine Learning Module}
\label{sec:ml}
As a complementary, purely data-driven approach to the signal/background separation used in the mass fits (Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:bayesian}), a supervised classifier was trained on weak labels derived from the mass sidebands and applied to study whether an ML-assisted event selection biases $\Araw$, and whether it can resolve local Dalitz-plot structure that a fixed binning would miss.

\subsection{Weak Labels and Feature Set}
\label{sec:ml_labels}
Weak labels were built from the same mass model as the Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_model}, $\mu=5284.4$, $\sigma=17.95\,\MeV$), without ever using the reconstructed $B$ mass itself as an input feature, to avoid mass sculpting. Events with $|m-\mu|<2\sigma$ (i.e.\ $m\in[5248.5,5320.3]\,\MeV$) are labeled signal-like; events with $|m-\mu|>4\sigma$ (outside $[5212.6,5356.2]\,\MeV$, within the fit window) are labeled background-like; the intermediate $2$--$4\sigma$ gap is left unlabeled and excluded, to keep the two classes cleanly separated. This gives $n_{\mathrm{labeled}}=16336$ events, of which $15119$ are signal-like and only $1217$ background-like (imbalance ratio $0.080$) -- a severe class imbalance, handled downstream with \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'} for the scikit-learn models, a sample-weight approximation for GBM (which has no native \texttt{class\_weight} support), and explicit class weights in the Keras loss for the FFNN.

The feature set consists of the 6 per-track kinematic/PID variables (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}, \texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}, \texttt{IPChi2}) for each of the 3 final-state tracks (18 features), plus two $B$-candidate-level variables found unused in the shared preselection and worth checking as classifier inputs: \texttt{B\_FlightDistance} and \texttt{B\_VertexChi2}, both standard LHCb combinatorial-background discriminants (a genuine $B$ flies further before decaying and has a better-reconstructed vertex than a random track combination). An exploratory check confirmed both separate the weak-label classes significantly (Kolmogorov-Smirnov $p<10^{-35}$ for each) and are not redundant with the existing features (Spearman $|\rho|<0.52$ with every one of the 18), so both were adopted, for $20$ features total. An exploratory PCA on the standardized features shows $15$ of $20$ components are needed to reach $90\%$ explained variance -- the features carry mostly independent information, so no dimensionality reduction is applied to the models used downstream (PCA is kept as a diagnostic only). The labeled sample was split $60/20/20$ into train/validation/test, stratified on the label, with the scaler fit on the training set only.

\subsection{Model Comparison}
\label{sec:ml_models}

\subsection{Regularization, Bias-Variance, and Calibration}
\label{sec:ml_regcal}
An $L1$/$L2$ regularization sweep on the logistic regression (log-spaced grid on $C=1/\alpha$, with CV) finds essentially identical optimal AUC for both penalties ($C_{L1}=0.024$, AUC $=0.7280$; $C_{L2}=45.2$, AUC $=0.7273$), with 3 of the 20 features driven to exactly zero by the $L1$ penalty (only 1 with 18 features) -- the linear model is still not meaningfully overfitting and gains little from aggressive regularization, consistent with its comparatively low AUC ceiling in Table~\ref{tab:ml_models}, though the larger feature set gives $L1$ slightly more to prune.

A bias-variance complexity curve was built for GBM using \texttt{staged\_predict} (train/test AUC as a function of the number of boosting stages). The train/test AUC gap grows from $0.014$ at $20$ trees to $0.033$ at the final iteration -- mild but measurable overfitting as complexity increases, though the gap stays small relative to the overall AUC scale ($\sim$0.81).

Calibration was assessed via the Brier score for all six models (Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} companion metric): Logistic Regression $0.208$, Logistic Regression (L1) $0.212$, Decision Tree $0.153$, Random Forest $0.132$, Gradient Boosting $0.125$, FFNN $0.071$. Notably the FFNN, despite the lowest AUC of the four non-linear models, has by far the best-calibrated raw score -- a model can rank events well (high AUC) without its output being a trustworthy probability, and vice versa. Applying isotonic recalibration (\texttt{CalibratedClassifierCV}) to the reference Random Forest model reduces its Brier score from $0.132$ to $0.053$ (a $60\%$ reduction): the RF score is a good \emph{ranking} but, uncalibrated, a poor \emph{probability} estimate.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:ml_uncertainty}
Two independent estimates of epistemic (model) uncertainty were computed per event: MC Dropout on the FFNN (dropout kept active at inference, $300$ repeated forward passes, uncertainty = std across passes) and ensemble-variance on the Random Forest (uncertainty = std of the individual trees' predictions, following the bagging-uncertainty prescription from Lecture~18). The two give mean uncertainties of $0.0112$ (MC Dropout) and $0.185$ (RF ensemble) -- a factor $\sim$16.5 apart in absolute scale.

Two checks were run to understand the discrepancy. First, whether the class imbalance (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}) inflates RF tree-to-tree variance on the rare background-like class: the mean RF std is $0.196$ on background-like test events vs.\ $0.185$ on signal-like -- only a $\sim$6\% relative difference, too small to explain a 16.5$\times$ gap. The same comparison for MC Dropout shows a much larger relative effect ($0.0224$ background vs.\ $0.0103$ signal, $\sim$2.2$\times$), suggesting the FFNN's epistemic uncertainty is more sensitive to the scarcity of background-like training examples than the RF's is. Second, whether the two methods at least agree on \emph{which} events are most uncertain, independent of absolute scale: the Spearman correlation between per-event MC-Dropout and RF-ensemble uncertainty is $\rho=0.368$ ($p=2.1\times10^{-105}$) -- highly significant and moderate, meaning the two epistemic-uncertainty estimates agree on relative event-level ranking more than with the 18-feature set ($\rho=0.24$ previously), but are still far from interchangeable.

\subsection{K-means on the Dalitz Plot}
\label{sec:ml_kmeans}
As an adaptive alternative to the fixed Dalitz-plot binning, $K$-means clustering ($K=8$, matching the fixed-binning granularity for direct comparison) was run on the standardized kinematic features. Repeating the clustering with different random seeds gives an adjusted Rand index of $0.996$ between runs, indicating a highly stable partition. Cluster sizes range from $n=1216$ to $n=4007$, and the local raw asymmetry $A_{\mathrm{local}}$ per cluster ranges from $-0.139$ to $+0.035$. Of the 8 clusters, exactly one shows a local deviation from zero exceeding $3\sigma$: cluster 2 ($n=3341$, $A_{\mathrm{local}}=-0.139$). Restricting that cluster to the high-purity signal window ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $n_{\mathrm{purified}}=2880$, $\sim$85\% purity) gives $A_{\mathrm{local}}=-0.147$ -- the asymmetry survives, and if anything strengthens slightly, after purification, disfavoring background contamination as its origin.

$K=8$ was chosen for direct comparability with the fixed binning, not from a clustering-quality criterion -- checked after the fact via elbow (inertia) and silhouette score over $K=2$--$12$. The global silhouette optimum is $K=3$ ($0.467$), but $K=8$ sits at a local silhouette maximum ($0.410$, higher than the neighboring $K=6,7$ and $K=9$--$12$), so it is not an arbitrary choice, just not the statistically optimal one -- a coarser, $K=3$ partition would likely be less directly comparable to the $8\times8$ fixed binning of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}.

\subsection{Stability of $A_{\mathrm{raw}}$ under ML Selection}
\label{sec:ml_stability}
The reference Random Forest score was applied to \emph{all} events in the fit window (not only the labeled subsample used for training), and the counting asymmetry $\Araw$ was recomputed after cutting at score thresholds from $0.1$ to $0.9$, with non-parametric bootstrap uncertainties (resampling the full selected sample and repeating cut+count) at each threshold. Across thresholds $0.1$--$0.7$, $\Araw$ stays in a narrow band, $[-0.054,-0.049]$, consistent within uncertainty with the nominal Bayesian fit result ($-0.0497$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}) and with bootstrap errors that are small and stable ($0.007$--$0.010$). At threshold $0.8$, $\Araw$ drifts to $-0.014$, but the bootstrap uncertainty there is also much larger ($0.026$), so the drift is only $\sim$1.4$\sigma$ from nominal -- suggestive but not significant. At threshold $0.9$ the selection becomes fully degenerate ($\Araw=-1$, every surviving event has the same charge, bootstrap uncertainty undefined) -- with 20 features the classifier is even more aggressive at extreme thresholds than with 18, leaving too few events to say anything statistically. Over the range where the selection retains a usable sample size, the ML-based event selection does not introduce a detectable bias on $\Araw$.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/ml_threshold_stability.png}
\caption{$\Araw\pm$bootstrap uncertainty vs.\ RF-score threshold, against the nominal Bayesian fit result (dotted line, shaded 68\% CI). Stable through threshold $0.7$; the collapse to $-1$ at threshold $0.9$ is visually obvious.}
\label{fig:ml_threshold}
\end{figure}

The threshold sweep above is simple post-cut counting, previously flagged as a limitation (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}) since it does not test whether the ML selection biases a genuine \emph{fit}-based estimate rather than just a count. This was addressed directly: the Bayesian mass model (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_model}) was refit -- same parametrization, same MCMC settings (32 walkers, 9000 steps) -- on the RF-selected subsample alone (score $>0.5$, $n=15204$), instead of counting. The result, $\Araw=-0.0493$ (68\% CI $[-0.058,-0.041]$), is consistent with both the nominal full-sample fit ($-0.0497$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}) and the simple-counting estimate on the same subsample ($-0.0499$) -- the ML selection does not bias a proper mass-fit estimate either, closing this limitation on the Bayesian side (the analogous frequentist-side comparison remains blocked on Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}).

As with the Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}), the ML-selected sample was also cross-checked by magnet polarity, to test whether the RF cut interacts with the detector's left-right instrumental asymmetry (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}) rather than tracking a genuine physical effect. At the representative RF-score threshold of $0.5$: $\Araw(\text{down})=-0.062\pm0.011$ ($n=8871$) and $\Araw(\text{up})=-0.032\pm0.013$ ($n=6333$). The two are individually somewhat more separated than in the Bayesian check ($\sim$1.8$\sigma$ apart here, vs.\ fully overlapping there), but follow the same sign pattern (down more negative than up) and their 68\% intervals still overlap -- consistent with the same underlying physical asymmetry seen through a smaller, ML-selected sample, not with a polarity-dependent artifact of the classifier.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several modeling choices and known weaknesses limit how far the results of Secs.~\ref{sec:bayesian}--\ref{sec:ml} can be pushed, and are collected here rather than scattered through the results sections.

On the Bayesian side (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayesian}): the background is assumed to split equally between $B$ charges -- checked both indirectly (magnet-polarity cross-check) and directly (sideband counting asymmetry, $-0.047\pm0.029$, compatible with zero at only $1.6\sigma$) in Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_syst}, but neither check has enough statistical power to fully rule out a real background asymmetry of similar size to the signal one; the exact value of the Savage-Dickey Bayes factor of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} is bandwidth-fragile by many orders of magnitude, and only its qualitative conclusion (decisive evidence against $A=0$) should be trusted; the toy-MC coverage check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_toymc} initially (60 replicas) showed a mild ($\sim$1.3$\sigma$) hint of undercoverage, which did not survive progressively larger replica counts (100: $\sim$1.1$\sigma$; 500: $\sim$0.8$\sigma$) -- resolved; even the best-fitting signal model (Crystal Ball with floated tail parameters) leaves $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}\approx2$, not $\approx1$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}); and the fixed-binning Dalitz analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz} uses a coarse $8\times8$ grid and a simple mass-window cut rather than a proper background subtraction.

On the machine-learning side (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml}): the weak labels suffer from severe class imbalance ($1217$ background-like vs.\ $15{,}119$ signal-like events, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}); the two epistemic-uncertainty estimators (MC Dropout, RF ensemble-variance) disagree by a factor $\sim$16.5 in absolute scale and are only moderately correlated event-by-event ($\rho\approx0.37$, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_uncertainty}); the GBM and FFNN hyperparameter/architecture searches (Tables~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams}--\ref{tab:ml_ffnn_search}) show a winner's-curse effect (best-by-CV configuration underperforms the untuned default on the test set) that widening the grid further would only worsen, not fix; and the $\Araw$-vs-threshold stability check (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_stability}) was originally simple post-cut counting only -- since addressed on the Bayesian side with a real mass refit on the ML-selected sample (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_stability}), but the equivalent frequentist-side refit still depends on Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}.

Cross-cutting the whole project: the mass window, background shape, and signal-region definition were fixed from the Bayesian fit alone and still need to be reconciled against Marco's frequentist choices before the comparison of Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} can be considered final; and no detector-level systematic correction (selection efficiency, production asymmetry) is applied anywhere in this work, so $\Araw$ as reported throughout is explicitly \emph{not} a measurement of $\ACP$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}).

The most natural next steps, in rough priority order, are: replacing the fixed-cut local Dalitz asymmetries (both binned, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}, and $K$-means, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans}) with a proper sWeight-based background subtraction; and running the equivalent frequentist-side post-selection mass refit on the ML-selected sample (the Bayesian-side refit is now done, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_stability}).

Both Tables~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams} and \ref{tab:ml_ffnn_search} illustrate the same effect for GBM and the FFNN: the configuration with the best \emph{cross-validated} score is not the one with the best \emph{test-set} score, and in both cases test performance is worse than the untuned default. This is not a bug but a known statistical phenomenon -- the "winner's curse" of hyperparameter selection: picking the arg-max over many (168 for the FFNN, 100--150 per model here) noisy cross-validation estimates systematically overestimates that configuration's true generalization performance, since the selection itself is biased toward whichever configuration got a favorable CV fluctuation. Searching an even wider grid would make this effect \emph{worse}, not better, since it widens the pool of candidates one of which can get "lucky" on CV -- the correct response is to report the CV/test gap honestly (as done here) rather than to keep expanding the search, and to treat the reported "best" configuration's absolute performance with some skepticism even though the underlying search procedure is not at fault. Random Forest does not show this effect (CV and test both improve together), consistent with the well-known property that Random Forest does not overfit as more trees are added -- unlike a hyperparameter grid search, which \emph{can} overfit to the validation metric given enough candidates.
