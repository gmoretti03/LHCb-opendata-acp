\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Charge-parity (CP) violation -- the small observed asymmetry between the behavior of matter and antimatter -- is one of the necessary ingredients for the matter-dominated universe we observe, yet the CP violation built into the Standard Model is far too small to account for it. Studying CP violation in the decays of $B$ mesons, where it is comparatively large and experimentally accessible, is one of the main ways to test the Standard Model's CKM description of the effect and search for physics beyond it. Charmless three-body decays such as $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ ($h=K,\pi$) are especially interesting in this respect: unlike a two-body decay, the three-body phase space (the Dalitz plot) allows the asymmetry to be studied not only as a single global number, but also \emph{locally}, since interference between intermediate resonances can concentrate CP-violating effects in specific kinematic regions rather than spreading them uniformly~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023,lhcb_cpv_paper}.

The quantity measured in this work is the raw asymmetry between the signal yields of the two charges,
\begin{equation}
\Araw=\frac{N(B^-)-N(B^+)}{N(B^-)+N(B^+)} .
\label{eq:araw}
\end{equation}
It differs from the physical asymmetry $\ACP$ by the production asymmetry $A_P$ of $B^\pm$ mesons in $pp$ collisions and by the detection asymmetry $A_D$ of the final state; to first order in these small quantities,
\begin{equation}
\ACP\approx\Araw-A_P-A_D ,
\label{eq:acp}
\end{equation}
where $A_D$ arises mainly from the different interaction cross sections of $K^+$ and $K^-$ with the detector material and depends on the kaon momenta~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023}. Neither correction is applied here, so $\Araw$ is not a measurement of $\ACP$. The production asymmetry of $B^\pm$ mesons at the LHC is expected at the level of one per cent; in the published analysis of the same 2011 data, the two corrections, measured with $B^\pm\to J/\psi K^\pm$ decays, together with an acceptance correction, move the asymmetry of $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ from $\Araw=-0.060\pm0.007$ to $\ACP=-0.043\pm0.009\,(\text{stat})\pm0.003\,(\text{syst})\pm0.007\,(J/\psi K)$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}.

In this work we use the LHCb Open Data record for $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$~\cite{opendata_record,opendata_notebook}, a subset of the 2011 data on which the first evidence of an inclusive $CP$ asymmetry in this decay was published~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}, to address the question: \emph{to what extent do frequentist likelihood fits, Bayesian inference, and machine-learning-assisted event selection produce consistent, well-controlled estimates of the raw asymmetry $\Araw$ in this decay?} The aim is not a competitive measurement of $\ACP$, but a methodological comparison of the three inference frameworks on the same data and the same physical question. Of the four channels in the record, $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ has the lowest background; the others ($\pi^\pm\pi^+\pi^-$, $K^\pm\pi^+\pi^-$, $\pi^\pm K^+K^-$) need a different particle-identification selection and a model of larger and peaking (misidentified) backgrounds, and are not analysed, to keep the focus on the comparison of the methods. The published $\ACP$ cannot be reproduced from this record, which does not contain the control channel used to correct $\Araw$ for the production and detection asymmetries (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}); what we extend is the statistical treatment: a common model fitted with two inference methods, a common systematic study, a validation of the intervals, and a machine-learning selection whose effect on the measurement is tested. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset and the preselection common to the three analyses; Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist} and \ref{sec:bayesian} present the frequentist and Bayesian fits, Sec.~\ref{sec:syst} their common systematic study, and Sec.~\ref{sec:ml} the machine-learning analysis; Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} brings the three together.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset and Preselection}
\label{sec:data_presel}
The dataset is the CERN Open Data record 4900, ``Matter Antimatter Differences -- B meson decays to three hadrons''~\cite{opendata_record}: candidates for $B^{\pm}\to h^{\pm}h^{+}h^{-}$ decays collected by LHCb in 2011 at $\sqrt{s}=7$\,TeV, reconstructed with the LHCb software and already passed through trigger and offline selection. The data are distributed as ROOT \texttt{TTree}s (\texttt{DecayTree}) containing only the most relevant reconstructed branches, split across two files by magnet polarity (magnet up/down), for a combined $8{,}556{,}118$ candidates. The same record also includes a simplified, detector-response-free phase-space simulation of $B^{\pm}\to K^{\pm}K^{+}K^{-}$ ($50{,}000$ decays, half of each charge); it is used only as a sample without asymmetry to test the local-asymmetry procedure (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}).

For each of the three final-state tracks ($H1$, $H2$, $H3$) the analysis reads momentum components (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}), particle-identification (PID) likelihoods (\texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}), a muon flag (\texttt{isMuon}), and the track charge. The same preselection is used in all three analyses: a kaon-PID requirement and a muon veto on every track,
\[
\texttt{H*\_ProbPi} < 0.5 \ \wedge\ \texttt{H*\_ProbK} > 0.5 \ \wedge\ \lnot\texttt{H*\_isMuon},
\]
which retains $23{,}895$ of the $8{,}556{,}118$ candidates ($0.28\%$), the PID requirement being by far the dominant reduction. The files contain no event identifier; as a proxy for multiple candidates from the same collision, only $42$ of the candidates in the fit window ($0.2\%$) share an identical track with another candidate, a negligible effect on the statistical uncertainties, and all are kept.

In this dataset only the charge combinations $(+1,-1,-1)$ and $(-1,+1,+1)$ occur for $(H1,H2,H3)$: $H1$ is always the kaon of charge opposite to the $B$, and $H2$, $H3$ are the interchangeable same-sign pair. The Dalitz-plot variables are therefore the invariant masses of the two opposite-sign pairs, $m^2(H1,H2)$ and $m^2(H1,H3)$.

\subsection{Invariant Mass Reconstruction}
\label{sec:data_mass}
The three final-state tracks are assigned the charged-kaon mass hypothesis ($M_K=493.677\,\MeV$) regardless of their PID likelihoods, and their four-momenta are summed to reconstruct the $B$ candidate, $m(KKK)=\sqrt{(\sum_i E_i)^2-|\sum_i \vec{p}_i|^2}$. The nominal fit window, common to the frequentist and Bayesian analyses, is $[5100,5600]\,\MeV$. Below $\sim$5150\,\MeV\ the background rises, consistent with a partially reconstructed contribution that a single exponential cannot describe; the window includes the start of this rise, which is described by a second exponential in the background model (Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:bayesian}). This leaves $19{,}731$ preselected candidates in the fit window: $10{,}292$ with $B$-charge $+1$ and $9439$ with $B$-charge $-1$, split $11{,}636$ (magnet down) / $8095$ (magnet up). The machine-learning analysis uses the same window, with the region below $5150\,\MeV$ excluded from its background-like training sample (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}).

\subsection{Data Exploration}

The spectrum peaks at $m\approx5284\,\MeV$ over a falling combinatorial background, with a similar shape for the two charges and the two magnet polarities. The charge-counting asymmetry of all candidates in the fit window, signal and background together, is $A_{\mathrm{count}}=(N^--N^+)/(N^-+N^+)=-0.043\pm0.007$ (binomial uncertainty).

\section{Bayesian Analysis}
\label{sec:bayesian}

\subsection{Model and Priors}
\label{sec:bayes_model}
The mass spectrum of each charge sample is described by a signal-plus-background mixture on the nominal fit window $[5100,5600]\,\MeV$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_mass}),
\begin{equation}
f^{\pm}(m)=N_s^{\pm}\,S(m;\mu,\sigma)+N_b^{\pm}\,B^{\pm}(m),
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is a Gaussian truncated to the window, with mean $\mu$ and width $\sigma$ shared by the two charges, and the background is a double exponential with a shape of its own for each charge,
\[
B^{\pm}(m)=f_{\pm}\,E(m;\lambda_{1}^{\pm})+(1-f_{\pm})\,E(m;\lambda_{2}^{\pm}),
\]
with $E(m;\lambda)\propto e^{-\lambda m}$ a truncated exponential normalized on the window. The steep component ($\lambda_1$) describes the rise of the background towards the lower edge of the window, the slow one ($\lambda_2$) the combinatorial background under and above the peak. The mixture is invariant under the exchange $(\lambda_1,f)\leftrightarrow(\lambda_2,1-f)$, which would give the posterior two identical modes (label switching); the ordering $\lambda_1>\lambda_2$ removes this symmetry. Signal and background yields are split between the charges as
\[
N_s^{\pm}=\tfrac{N_s}{2}(1\mp A),\qquad N_b^{\pm}=\tfrac{N_b}{2}(1\mp A_b),
\]
so that $A=(N_s^--N_s^+)/(N_s^-+N_s^+)$, the raw asymmetry \Araw of Eq.~\eqref{eq:araw}, is a fit parameter rather than being derived from two independent yields, and the background asymmetry $A_b$ is free. The parameter vector has twelve components, $\theta=(N_s,N_b,A,A_b,\mu,\sigma,\lambda_1^{\pm},\lambda_2^{\pm},f_{\pm})$.

Given the data $D=\{(m_i,q_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ (mass and charge of each candidate), Bayes' theorem gives the posterior
\begin{equation}
p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{p(D)},
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
with the extended unbinned likelihood of the two charge samples
\begin{multline}
\mathcal{L}(D\mid\theta)\propto e^{-(N_s+N_b)}
\prod_{i:\,q_i=+1}\Big[N_s^{+}S(m_i)+N_b^{+}B^{+}(m_i)\Big] \\
\times\prod_{i:\,q_i=-1}\Big[N_s^{-}S(m_i)+N_b^{-}B^{-}(m_i)\Big],
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{multline}
where $S(m_i)\equiv S(m_i;\mu,\sigma)$. The priors are independent and uniform: $N_s,N_b\in(0,5N)$ with $N=19{,}731$, $A,A_b\in(-1,1)$, $\mu\in(5260,5310)\,\MeV$, $\sigma\in(3,40)\,\MeV$, $\lambda_1^{\pm}\in(10^{-5},0.1)\,\MeV^{-1}$, $\lambda_2^{\pm}\in(10^{-5},\lambda_1^{\pm})$, $f_{\pm}\in(0,1)$. The prior density is therefore a constant on the allowed region $\Theta$,
\begin{equation}
\pi(\theta)\propto\mathbf{1}\left[\theta\in\Theta\right],
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
and inside it the posterior is proportional to the likelihood. A flat prior is not uninformative in an absolute sense, since it is not invariant under reparametrization (a prior uniform in $\lambda$ is not uniform in $1/\lambda$); with $\sim$$2\times10^4$ events, however, the posterior is dominated by the likelihood, as the prior-sensitivity check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks} confirms for $A$. The evidence $p(D)$ is a constant normalization: the MCMC samples $\mathcal{L}\,\pi$ without computing it, and the Savage-Dickey ratio of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} avoids computing it as well. The marginal posterior of the asymmetry, $p(A\mid D)=\int p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta_{\neq A}$, is the histogram of the $A$ component of the chain.

\subsection{MCMC Sampling and Convergence Diagnostics}
\label{sec:bayes_mcmc}
The posterior was sampled with the affine-invariant ensemble sampler of Goodman and Weare~\cite{goodman_weare} as implemented in \texttt{emcee}~\cite{emcee}. The maximum of the posterior was first located numerically (Nelder-Mead), and 32 walkers were initialized in a narrow ball around it and run for $25{,}000$ steps, with the first $4166$ ($1/6$) discarded as burn-in, leaving $6.7\times10^5$ samples; the mean acceptance fraction is $0.38$. Convergence was checked through the integrated autocorrelation time of each parameter, $\tau=1+2\sum_{t\geq1}\rho(t)$, with $\rho(t)$ the autocorrelation of the chain at lag $t$. We find $\tau\in[147,183]$ steps, the longest for the background-shape parameters: the post-burn-in chain is $N/\tau\geq114$ autocorrelation times long for every parameter, above the threshold of $50$ recommended for a reliable estimate of $\tau$~\cite{emcee}, and corresponds to $\gtrsim3600$ effectively independent samples per parameter. To check that the chain is not trapped near its starting point, the fit was repeated twice with the walkers initialized uniformly over the whole prior box and the first half of the chain discarded: both runs converge to the same single mode, with $\Araw=-0.0446$ and $-0.0442$, the same intervals and the same values of all other parameters as the nominal fit. The trace plot of such a run (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_trace}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}) shows all walkers reaching the bulk of the posterior within $\sim$2000 steps, the background fraction $f_+$ last, well inside the discarded half of the chain.

\subsection{Posterior Results and Credible Intervals}
\label{sec:bayes_posterior}
The marginal posterior on the raw asymmetry gives (median and equal-tailed credible intervals, CI)
\begin{gather*}
\Araw = -0.0443, \\
68\%~\text{CI}~[-0.0528,-0.0358], \\ 95\%~\text{CI}~[-0.0608,-0.0276].
\end{gather*}
The posterior is very close to Gaussian, with standard deviation $0.0085$; its 68\% interval is symmetric about the median to within $2\times10^{-5}$, so the equal-tailed intervals coincide with the highest-posterior-density ones at the quoted precision. Since the priors are uniform, the posterior mode (the maximum a posteriori, MAP, estimate, $-0.0441$) coincides with the maximum-likelihood estimate, so this result can be compared directly with the frequentist fit of the same model (Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}). The published analysis of the same 2011 data, with its own selection (which includes a $D^0$ veto) and a mass model with partially reconstructed and misidentified backgrounds, finds $\Araw=-0.060\pm0.007$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}; with the $D^0$ veto our fit gives $-0.0496\pm0.0088$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}), compatible within the statistical uncertainties, although the two selections overlap and differ. After the corrections of Eq.~\eqref{eq:acp}, the published asymmetry is $\ACP=-0.043\pm0.009\pm0.003\pm0.007$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}, and the most precise later measurement, on a larger data set, is $\ACP=-0.037\pm0.002\pm0.002\pm0.003$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023,lhcb_cpv_paper}. The other physics parameters are $N_s=15{,}328\pm133$, $N_b=4404\pm82$, $A_b=-0.040\pm0.018$, $\mu=5284.39\pm0.16\,\MeV$ and $\sigma=18.00\pm0.14\,\MeV$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_corner}). $A$ is almost uncorrelated with the other parameters: its largest correlation, $-0.17$, is with $A_b$.

\subsection{Bayes Factors}
\label{sec:bayes_factor}
Computing the evidence of each hypothesis by direct numerical integration is impractical for a 12-parameter posterior. When the null hypothesis fixes one parameter, $H_0$: $A=0$, nested in $H_1$ ($A$ free), and the priors on the other parameters are shared, the Bayes factor reduces to the Savage-Dickey density ratio~\cite{dickey1971},
\[
BF_{01} = \frac{p(A=0\mid D)}{\pi(A=0)}, \qquad \pi(A=0)=\tfrac12 .
\]
With equal prior probabilities for the two hypotheses, $BF_{01}$ is also the posterior odds of $H_0$ against $H_1$. $A=0$ lies $5.2$ posterior standard deviations from the median, in a tail where the chain contains essentially no samples, so a kernel density estimate of $p(A=0\mid D)$ would only reflect the kernel tails of the few most extreme samples. We therefore use the Gaussian approximation of the marginal posterior (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}), with the mean and standard deviation of the chain, which gives $BF_{01}\approx1.1\times10^{-4}$ ($\ln BF_{10}\approx9.1$): decisive evidence against $A=0$ on Jeffreys' scale~\cite{jeffreys} ($BF_{10}>100$). As for any Bayes factor of a point null against a free parameter, the value scales with the prior width -- the Ockham factor built into the Bayes factor, which penalizes a free parameter in proportion to the prior volume it is allowed to explore, and the mechanism behind Lindley's paradox: with the narrower prior $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}, $\pi(A=0)$ grows to $1/0.6$ and $BF_{01}$ drops by a factor $3.3$, which leaves the conclusion unchanged. Figure~\ref{fig:bayes_sd} shows the two densities of the ratio.

The same test on the background asymmetry shows the Ockham factor at work. $A_b=-0.040\pm0.018$ lies $2.3$ standard deviations from zero, yet the Savage-Dickey ratio gives $BF_{01}(A_b=0)\approx3.5$: the data mildly \emph{favor} a charge-symmetric background, because the gain in likelihood does not compensate for spreading the prior of $A_b$ over $(-1,1)$. A negative background asymmetry is plausible: besides combinatorial background, the sample contains misidentified $B^\pm\to K^\pm\pi^+\pi^-$ and $B^\pm\to\pi^\pm K^+K^-$ decays, with $\ACP$ values of their own, whose contribution grows when the kaon identification is loosened (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}). $A_b$ is nevertheless kept free in the nominal model, which avoids imposing an assumption on the background that the frequentist analysis does not make; fixing $A_b=0$ is one of the variants of the systematic study (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}).

\subsection{Goodness of Fit and Model Choice}
\label{sec:bayes_gof}
A binned comparison of data and posterior-median model ($\chi^2=\sum(\text{obs}-\text{exp})^2/\text{exp}$ over 50 bins of $10\,\MeV$ per charge, $100-12=88$ degrees of freedom) gives $\chi^2=164.1/88$ ($p=1.6\times10^{-6}$), with $95.4$ for $B^+$ and $68.7$ for $B^-$. The largest pulls, in the $B^+$ sample, sit on the two flanks of the peak, about $\pm3\sigma$ from $\mu$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_fit}): the observed signal shape has heavier tails than a Gaussian. Replacing the Gaussian by a Crystal Ball shape~\cite{skwarnicki}, a Gaussian core with a power-law low-mass tail,
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{CB}}(x)\propto
\begin{cases}
e^{-x^2/2}, & x>-\beta,\\[2pt]
\left(\frac{\kappa}{\beta}\right)^{\kappa} e^{-\beta^2/2}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\beta}-\beta-x\right)^{-\kappa}, & x\leq-\beta,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:cb}
\end{equation}
with $x=(m-\mu)/\sigma$, $\beta$ the point where the tail starts (in units of $\sigma$) and $\kappa$ its power, both free, improves the fit to $\chi^2=131.0/86$ ($p=1.3\times10^{-3}$, $\beta=1.71$, $\kappa=35$) but moves $\Araw$ by only $-0.0002$ (Table~\ref{tab:gof}). The asymmetry does not depend on the signal shape even though none of the models reaches $\chi^2/\text{ndof}\approx1$. The Gaussian is nevertheless kept as the nominal signal model, common to the two analyses: the Crystal Ball adds two tail parameters that are poorly constrained (its power $\kappa$ runs to large values), and the effect of the signal shape on $\Araw$ is covered by the systematic study (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}).

The background model is constrained by the data much more strongly. A single exponential per charge is rejected ($\chi^2=297.7/92$, $\Delta\chi^2=134$ for four fewer parameters), and so it is not used even as a systematic variant (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}). A shape shared between the two charges ($\Delta\chi^2=9.9$ for three fewer parameters) and a charge-symmetric background ($\Delta\chi^2=5.7$ for one fewer) are acceptable descriptions and shift $\Araw$ by $-0.0037$ and $-0.0028$: the background treatment is the largest model dependence of the fit. The lower edge of the window is a compromise between two failures. Extending the window to $5050\,\MeV$ degrades the fit ($\chi^2=217.4/98$), because the double exponential no longer follows the background further below the peak; raising it to $5150\,\MeV$ removes most of the region that constrains the steep component, which becomes poorly identified -- the chain for its fraction $f_+$ mixes slowly ($N/\tau=10$) -- while $\Araw$ itself is unchanged ($-0.0444$).

The binned $\chi^2$ uses the posterior medians only. A posterior predictive check~\cite{gelman_ppc} accounts for the posterior uncertainty and tests specific features of the data: for $1000$ parameter sets $\theta_j$ drawn from the posterior, a dataset $y^{\mathrm{rep}}_j$ is generated from the model, and a discrepancy $T(y,\theta_j)$ is computed both on the replica and on the data; the posterior predictive $p$-value is $p_B=P\big(T(y^{\mathrm{rep}},\theta)\geq T(y,\theta)\mid y\big)$. For the binned $\chi^2$ the replicas give $104\pm15$ and the data $175$ (median over $\theta_j$): none of the replicas reaches the observed value ($p_B<10^{-3}$), with the misfit concentrated in the $B^+$ sample ($p_B<10^{-3}$, against $0.03$ for $B^-$). The discrepancies that probe the charge structure of the model are instead reproduced: the counting asymmetry of the whole window ($p_B=0.996$), of the signal window ($0.99$) and of the sidebands ($0.73$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_ppc}). The model misdescribes the shape of the spectrum, mainly the signal tails, but not the split between the charges that determines $\Araw$, consistently with the stability of $\Araw$ under changes of the signal model.

\subsection{Cross-Checks}
\label{sec:bayes_checks}
The fit was repeated separately on the two magnet polarities, whose left-right detection asymmetries are opposite and partially cancel in the combined sample: $\Araw(\text{down})=-0.0497\pm0.0112$ ($n=11{,}636$) and $\Araw(\text{up})=-0.0378\pm0.0129$ ($n=8095$). They differ by $0.7\sigma$, so there is no indication that the asymmetry is an artifact of one polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_magnet}). A narrower prior, $A\in(-0.3,0.3)$, leaves the posterior unchanged ($\Araw=-0.0443$), as expected for a posterior far from the prior bounds; the prior width matters only for the Bayes factor (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor}).

The sample contains $B^\pm$ decays through intermediate charm states, $B^\pm\to D^0K^\pm$, $J/\psi K^\pm$ and $\chi_{c0}K^\pm$ with $D^0$, $J/\psi$, $\chi_{c0}\to K^+K^-$, which are visible as peaks in the opposite-sign $m(K^+K^-)$ spectra. They are part of the $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ final state and are kept in the nominal sample; removing them changes the observable, from the asymmetry of all $B^\pm\to K^\pm K^+K^-$ decays to that of the charmless ones, so the vetoes are reported as an informative check rather than as a systematic variation. A candidate is vetoed if either opposite-sign pair, $m(H1,H2)$ or $m(H1,H3)$, lies within $\pm30\,\MeV$ of the $D^0$ or $J/\psi$ mass or within $\pm60\,\MeV$ of the $\chi_{c0}$ mass (world-average masses; the wider window for the $\chi_{c0}$ reflects its natural width). The $D^0$ veto alone keeps $18{,}744$ candidates and gives $\Araw=-0.0496\pm0.0088$; the three vetoes together keep $16{,}367$ and give $-0.0423\pm0.0094$. The shifts with respect to the nominal fit, $-0.0053$ and $+0.0020$, compare with expected statistical fluctuations of $0.0023$ and $0.0040$ for a subsample of the nominal one (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}); the $D^0$ veto moves $\Araw$ by $2.3$ times this amount, i.e.\ the vetoed $D^0K^\pm$ candidates have a less negative asymmetry than the rest of the sample.

\subsection{Toy-MC Coverage Validation}
\label{sec:bayes_toymc}
A credible interval states where the parameter lies given the data and the prior; nothing guarantees that it also has frequentist coverage, i.e.\ that over repeated experiments it contains the true value with the stated probability. To check this, 200 pseudo-datasets were generated from the fitted model (parametric bootstrap, with the posterior medians as ground truth and Poisson-fluctuated yields for each charge and component; the truncated Gaussian and exponential masses are drawn with the transformation method, i.e.\ by inverting their cumulative distributions) and each was refit with the nominal procedure and a $12{,}000$-step chain. The fraction of replicas whose interval contains the injected $A$ is $0.650\pm0.033$ for the 68\% CI and $0.930\pm0.015$ for the 95\% CI (binomial uncertainties), $1.0\sigma$ and $1.3\sigma$ below nominal: the coverage is consistent with nominal. The estimates themselves are unbiased and their spread matches the reported uncertainty (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_toys}): their mean differs from the injected value by $-0.0002\pm0.0006$, their standard deviation, $0.0088$, agrees with the posterior standard deviation of the data fit, $0.0085$, and the pulls have mean $-0.03\pm0.07$ and standard deviation $1.04$.

The toy chains are shorter than the nominal one, and in 124 of the 200 replicas the slowest-mixing parameter, always a background-shape parameter, has $N/\tau<50$ (median $48$, minimum $22$). The criterion on the slowest parameter is conservative for the quantity tested here: for $A$ itself the median autocorrelation time is $156$ steps, so each chain is at least $45$ autocorrelation times long in $A$ and provides $\gtrsim1400$ effectively independent samples of it. The Monte Carlo error on the percentiles of $A$ is then a few per cent of $\sigma(A)$, negligible against the binomial uncertainty of the coverage, and the replicas with $N/\tau<50$ do not show a lower coverage than the others. This is a closure test of the inference procedure: since the toys are generated from the same model that is fitted, it does not probe the model mismatch of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}, which is instead addressed by the systematic study.

\subsection{Local Dalitz-Plot Asymmetry (Fixed Binning)}
\label{sec:bayes_dalitz}
To test whether the asymmetry is uniform over phase space or concentrated in specific regions, each candidate was placed in the Dalitz plane using the two opposite-sign $K^+K^-$ invariant masses $m^2(H1,H2)$ and $m^2(H1,H3)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_presel}), sorted into $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ and $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ to remove the $H2/H3$ labeling ambiguity. The plane $[0,16]\times[0,30]\,\GeV^2$ in $(m^2_{\mathrm{lo}},m^2_{\mathrm{hi}})$ was divided into an $8\times8$ grid, fixed before looking at the asymmetries. In each bin with at least 20 candidates (32 of 64), the local asymmetry $A_i=(N_i^--N_i^+)/N_i$, with binomial uncertainty $\sigma(A_i)=\sqrt{(1-A_i^2)/N_i}$, was compared with the global counting asymmetry $A_{\mathrm{glob}}=-0.043$ through $S_i=(A_i-A_{\mathrm{glob}})/\sigma(A_i)$; a comparison with zero would instead mostly reflect the global asymmetry itself.

Two of the 32 bins have $|S_i|>3$, adjacent to each other at $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}<4\,\GeV^2$ and $7.5<m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}<11.25\,\GeV^2$ ($S_i=-3.7$ and $-3.0$), with $A_i$ more negative than the global value; the bin below the first one ($m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}<2$, $3.75<m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}<7.5\,\GeV^2$) has $S_i=-2.98$, just under the threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_dalitz}); about $0.09$ would be expected if the asymmetry were uniform, with a probability of $3\times10^{-3}$ for two or more. The largest positive deviations, close to $+3$, lie at high $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$. A global test of homogeneity, $\chi^2=\sum_i S_i^2=80.8$ for 31 degrees of freedom ($p=3\times10^{-6}$), confirms that the local asymmetry is not uniform. Restricting the sample to the signal window ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $n=15{,}130$, where the fitted model gives a signal purity of $\approx$96\%) gives the same picture -- three bins with $|S_i|>3$, $\chi^2=95.6/28$ -- so the structure is not produced by the combinatorial background. Nor is it produced by the charm contributions: with the $D^0$ veto two bins still exceed $3\sigma$ ($\chi^2=79.2/31$), and with all three vetoes one remains ($\chi^2=74.4/31$, $p=2\times10^{-5}$). It does not depend on the binning either: $6\times6$, $10\times10$ and $12\times12$ grids give $\chi^2=81.0/19$, $97.1/43$ and $120.0/60$ ($p\leq7\times10^{-6}$).

Two null tests check the procedure itself. Shuffling the charges among the candidates removes any local asymmetry while keeping the occupancy of the bins and the global asymmetry of the data: over $2000$ permutations the $8\times8$ grid gives $\chi^2=31.4\pm8.1$, as expected for 31 degrees of freedom, and no permutation reaches the observed value (permutation $p<5\times10^{-4}$; $p=1\times10^{-3}$ for the $12\times12$ grid). The phase-space simulation of the record, without asymmetry by construction, gives $\chi^2=40.0/30$ ($p=0.11$) with the same procedure. The homogeneity test therefore has its nominal behavior, and the non-uniformity is a property of the data.

The structure does not depend on the magnet polarity either. The left-right part of the detection asymmetry changes sign when the polarity is reversed, so a local asymmetry produced by it would appear with opposite deviations in the two samples. In the region of the two significant bins ($m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}<4\,\GeV^2$, $7.5<m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}<11.25\,\GeV^2$) the counting asymmetry is $-0.129\pm0.023$ with the magnet down and $-0.126\pm0.028$ with the magnet up, although the global asymmetries of the two samples differ ($-0.051$ and $-0.032$), and the two maps agree bin by bin ($\chi^2=23.0$ for 29 bins, $p=0.78$). This split does not test the part of the detection asymmetry that does not change sign with the polarity, the different interaction of $K^+$ and $K^-$ with the detector material.

The ordered Dalitz plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:dalitz_region}, left) shows the two-body resonances of the decay: the narrow $\phi(1020)$ band at the kinematic edge, $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}\approx1.04\,\GeV^2$, and the charm bands of the $D^0$, $J/\psi$ and $\chi_{c0}$. The negative local asymmetry is not produced by the $\phi(1020)$: its band ($|m_{\mathrm{lo}}-m_\phi|<10\,\MeV$, $1964$ candidates) has $A_{\mathrm{count}}=-0.004\pm0.023$, while the significant bins without it have $-0.145\pm0.019$. The asymmetry sits in the low-mass $K^+K^-$ region just above the $\phi$, which is where the published analysis of the same data located it, defining the region $1.2<m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}<2.0\,\GeV^2$, $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}<15\,\GeV^2$ and measuring there $\Araw=-0.239\pm0.020$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_2013}. Since that region is fixed by the paper and not chosen on our data, it allows an unbiased comparison: fitting the $2146$ candidates in it with the nominal model gives $\Araw=-0.234\pm0.025$ (Bayesian; the chain mixes slowly in the poorly constrained second background exponential, $N/\tau=15$) and $-0.237\pm0.025$ (frequentist, $9.4\sigma$ from zero), reproducing the published value; the mass spectra of the two charges in the region (Fig.~\ref{fig:dalitz_region}, right) show the difference directly. Three caveats apply: the binning is coarse and was not optimized; the asymmetries are computed on all candidates, without the sWeight-based background subtraction that a full analysis would use (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}); and, since $\Araw$ is not corrected for detection asymmetries, local variations can still be partly instrumental -- the $K^+$/$K^-$ interaction asymmetry depends on the kaon momenta, which are correlated with the position in the Dalitz plane, and is not removed by the polarity split. Section~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans} revisits the same question with an adaptive, data-driven partition ($K$-means).

\subsection{Bayesian and Frequentist Intervals at Low Statistics}
\label{sec:bayes_lowstat}
With $\sim$$2\times10^4$ candidates the posterior is Gaussian and, with flat priors, the Bayesian and frequentist results coincide by construction (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}). To find where they could differ, the same likelihood was analysed with both methods on pseudo-datasets of $N=50$, $150$, $500$ and $2000$ candidates, $300$ for each size. The model is the six-parameter one of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_minimal} (Gaussian signal, one exponential background shared by the charges), which stays identifiable down to a few tens of events; the parameters are those of its fit to the data, with the yields rescaled. Each replica is analysed with the MCMC (median and equal-tailed credible intervals) and by maximum likelihood with \texttt{iminuit} (Hesse intervals, and Minos intervals from the profile likelihood and Wilks' theorem).

The two methods agree at every size (Fig.~\ref{fig:bayes_lowstat}). The coverages of the three intervals are compatible with nominal and with each other, the posterior median and the maximum-likelihood estimate differ on average by $5\%$ of the statistical uncertainty at $N=50$ and by less above, and the credible intervals stay symmetric. The asymmetry is a ratio of Poisson yields, whose likelihood is close to Gaussian already with a few tens of signal events: in this problem flat-prior credible intervals and profile-likelihood confidence intervals give the same answer, and Wilks' theorem holds, down to $N=50$. The two approaches differ in what the interval means and in hypothesis testing (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor}), not in the numbers. At $N=2000$ all three intervals over-cover slightly ($0.98$--$0.99$ for the 95\% interval); since the effect is the same for the three methods, it is a feature of this set of replicas rather than of either inference.

\subsection{A Minimal Model as Cross-Check}
\label{sec:bayes_minimal}
Before the nominal model was fixed, the same analysis was carried out with a minimal six-parameter model: a narrower window, $[5150,5450]\,\MeV$, which excludes most of the rising background below the peak, a single exponential background with a shape and a yield shared by the two charges, and the same Gaussian signal. On its $17{,}839$ candidates it gives $\Araw=-0.0497$ (68\% CI $[-0.058,-0.041]$) with $\chi^2/\text{ndof}=127.4/54$, and $-0.0457$ when the background yields of the two charges are left free. Its toy validation, with 500 pseudo-datasets, gives coverage consistent with nominal (0.664 and 0.962 for the 68\% and 95\% intervals). The difference from the nominal result, $0.005$, is driven by the background treatment, as in the systematic study of Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}, and is smaller than the statistical uncertainty.

\section{Systematic Uncertainties}
\label{sec:syst}
The frequentist and Bayesian fits share one systematic study: the same thirteen variants of the nominal choices, in four groups, and the same rule to combine them. Each variant is fitted by both methods on exactly the same candidates.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Window}: lower edge at $5050$ or $5150\,\MeV$, upper edge at $5450$ or $5700\,\MeV$.
\item \emph{Background}: a single exponential per charge; a double exponential with the shape shared between charges; a charge-symmetric background ($A_b=0$).
\item \emph{Signal}: a Crystal Ball shape with free tail parameters, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cb}; $\mu$ and $\sigma$ separate for the two charges.
\item \emph{Preselection}: one cut at a time loosened or tightened, $\texttt{ProbK}>0.4$ or $>0.6$, $\texttt{ProbPi}<0.4$ or $<0.6$, on all three tracks.
\end{itemize}

The shift $\Delta=\Araw^{\mathrm{var}}-\Araw^{\mathrm{nom}}$ of a variant is not necessarily a systematic effect. When a variant changes the sample (window, preselection), the two samples are nested and part of the shift is a statistical fluctuation, with expected size $\sigma_B=\sqrt{|\sigma_{\mathrm{var}}^2-\sigma_{\mathrm{nom}}^2|}$, $\sigma$ being the statistical uncertainty of each fit~\cite{barlow}; such a variant contributes only if $|\Delta|>\sigma_B$. A variant that the data reject, with a $\chi^2$ worse than the nominal one by more than $25$ on the same data, is not a plausible alternative and is excluded: this removes the single exponential ($\Delta\chi^2=134$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}). The largest remaining $|\Delta|$ of each group is taken as its uncertainty, since the variants of a group are alternative descriptions of the same choice, and the four groups are added in quadrature.

The preselection dominates, through the single variant $\texttt{ProbK}>0.6$ ($+0.0058$, $1.2\,\sigma_B$). On the tight side of the kaon requirement the shifts scatter around zero without a trend ($+0.0009$, $+0.0058$, $-0.0017$, $-0.0112$, $-0.0054$, $+0.0010$, $+0.0022$ for $\texttt{ProbK}>0.55$--$0.85$, all within $1.3\,\sigma_B$), and the pion requirement gives a flat scan within $1.3\,\sigma_B$: the $\texttt{ProbK}>0.6$ value is a statistical fluctuation, and the preselection uncertainty is a conservative estimate. Each method computes $\sigma_B$ from its own uncertainties (posterior standard deviation, Hesse error). Since $\sigma_B$ is the difference of two nearly equal uncertainties, it is sensitive to small differences between them, including the Monte Carlo noise of the posterior standard deviation (1--3\%), and a variant close to the threshold can be classified differently by the two methods: $\texttt{ProbPi}<0.4$ counts for the Bayesian fit ($|\Delta|=0.00223$, $\sigma_B=0.00220$) and not for the frequentist one ($0.00204$ vs $0.00229$). This does not affect the result, since the uncertainty of each group is set by its largest shift, the same in both methods.

\section{Machine Learning Module}
\label{sec:ml}
As a complementary, data-driven approach to the signal/background separation of the mass fits (Secs.~\ref{sec:frequentist}--\ref{sec:bayesian}), supervised classifiers were trained on weak labels derived from the mass spectrum, and used to study whether an ML-assisted event selection changes $\Araw$ or the mass spectrum, and whether an adaptive partition of the Dalitz plane resolves local structure.

\subsection{Weak Labels and Feature Set}
\label{sec:ml_labels}
No candidate carries a true signal/background label, so the classifiers are trained with weak labels, following the classification-without-labels (CWoLa) approach~\cite{cwola}: a classifier is trained to separate two \emph{mixed} samples with different signal fractions, here a signal-enriched window around the $B$ peak and the mass sidebands. If, within each class, the feature distributions are the same in the two samples, the optimal classifier between the two mixtures is a monotonic function of the signal-to-background likelihood ratio $p_s(x)/p_b(x)$, which by the Neyman-Pearson lemma is the most powerful test statistic for signal against background: the classifier trained on the mixtures is also the optimal signal/background classifier~\cite{cwola}. Two conditions follow. The background in the sidebands must be representative of the background under the peak; and the features must not be correlated with the $B$ mass. The second condition cannot hold exactly: the reconstructed mass is not an input feature, but the nine momentum components of the three kaons determine it, so a flexible model can partly learn the mass, separate the two mass regions rather than signal from background, and \emph{sculpt} the mass distribution of the candidates that pass a cut on its score. Section~\ref{sec:ml_stability} tests this directly.

The labels use the nominal Bayesian fit (Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_posterior}, $\mu=5284.39$, $\sigma=18.00\,\MeV$). Candidates with $|m-\mu|<2\sigma$, $m\in[5248.4,5320.4]\,\MeV$, are labeled signal-like ($15{,}130$); candidates in $[5150,\mu-4\sigma]\cup[\mu+4\sigma,5600]=[5150,5212.4]\cup[5356.4,5600]\,\MeV$ are labeled background-like ($1940$); the $2$--$4\sigma$ gap is left unlabeled. The region $5100$--$5150\,\MeV$, inside the fit window, is excluded from the background-like class to satisfy the first condition: there the background rises towards partially reconstructed $B$ decays (Sec.~\ref{sec:data_mass}), whose kinematics differ from those of the combinatorial background under the peak. The classes are imbalanced ($1940/15{,}130=0.128$). Every model is trained with the same balanced class weights, $w_c=N/(2N_c)$, i.e.\ $4.40$ for background-like and $0.56$ for signal-like candidates (through \texttt{class\_weight} for the scikit-learn models and the network, and through sample weights for gradient boosting), so that the two classes contribute equally to the loss.

The feature set consists of the 6 per-track kinematic/PID variables (\texttt{PX}, \texttt{PY}, \texttt{PZ}, \texttt{ProbK}, \texttt{ProbPi}, \texttt{IPChi2}) for each of the 3 final-state tracks (18 features), plus two $B$-candidate variables not used in the preselection: \texttt{B\_FlightDistance} and \texttt{B\_VertexChi2}, standard discriminants against combinatorial background (a genuine $B$ flies further before decaying and has a better-reconstructed vertex than a random track combination). Both separate the weak-label classes (Kolmogorov-Smirnov $p<10^{-60}$) and are not redundant with the per-track features (Spearman $|\rho|\leq0.53$ with each of the 18), giving $20$ features. A principal-component analysis of the standardized features needs $15$ of $20$ components to reach $90\%$ of the variance, so no dimensionality reduction is applied. The labeled sample was split $60/20/20$ into training, validation and test sets ($10{,}242/3414/3414$ candidates, $388$ background-like in the test set), stratified on the label, with the feature scaler fit on the training set only.

\subsection{Model Comparison}
\label{sec:ml_models}
Six classifiers were trained: logistic regression, without and with an $L1$ penalty (linear baselines), a decision tree (depth 4), a Random Forest~\cite{breiman_rf} (300 trees of depth 8), gradient boosting~\cite{friedman_gbm} (200 stages of depth-3 trees, learning rate $0.05$), and a feed-forward neural network (FFNN: two hidden layers of 32 and 16 units with ReLU activation, dropout $0.2$, binary cross-entropy, the Adam optimizer~\cite{adam} with learning rate $10^{-3}$, early stopping on the validation AUC). The models are compared through the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), which equals the probability that a randomly chosen signal-like candidate receives a higher score than a randomly chosen background-like one ($0.5$ for a random classifier, $1$ for a perfect one). Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} gives the 5-fold cross-validated AUC on the training set and the AUC on the untouched test set, with its bootstrap uncertainty. The reference model for the rest of the section was chosen on the cross-validated AUC, so that the test set is not used for any choice: gradient boosting ($0.866$), essentially tied with the Random Forest ($0.863$).

The two measures of the relevance of a feature available here -- the Random Forest importance (the decrease in Gini impurity $G=1-\sum_c p_c^2$, $p_c$ the class fractions in a node, produced by the splits on that feature) and the magnitude of the $L1$-regularized (Lasso~\cite{tibshirani_lasso}) logistic-regression coefficient of the standardized feature -- agree only partly (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_features}; Spearman correlation of the two rankings $0.42$, $p=0.06$). \subsection{Regularization, Bias-Variance, and Calibration}
\label{sec:ml_regcal}
A scan of the regularization strength of the logistic regression ($30$ log-spaced values of $C=1/\alpha$, cross-validated) gives the same optimal AUC for the $L1$ and $L2$ penalties ($0.7686$, at $C_{L1}=0.12$ and $C_{L2}=9.2$), and the optimal $L1$ penalty sets no coefficient to zero: the linear model does not overfit, and its limitation is the linear decision boundary, not the variance.

The bias-variance trade-off was studied with gradient boosting, whose complexity grows with the number of boosting stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_bias_variance}). The test AUC rises up to $\sim$150 stages and then stays at $0.865$, while the training AUC keeps rising: the train-test gap grows from $0.018$ at 20 stages to $0.084$ at 300. The additional stages fit fluctuations of the training sample without improving the ranking of new candidates -- overfitting, which the default 200 stages of Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} do not reach in the test AUC. The accuracy at the $0.5$ threshold stays below that of always predicting the majority class ($0.886$): with balanced class weights the classifier is trained to minimize the weighted error, in which a misclassified background-like candidate counts eight times more than a signal-like one, and not the unweighted error rate.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/ml_nominal_bias_variance.pdf}
\caption{Gradient boosting: accuracy at the $0.5$ threshold (left) and AUC (right) on the training and test sets as a function of the number of boosting stages. The dotted line is the accuracy of always predicting the signal-like class.}
\label{fig:ml_bias_variance}
\end{figure*}

The AUC depends only on the ranking of the scores; whether a score can be read as a probability is measured by the Brier score~\cite{brier}, $\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (p_i-y_i)^2$, the mean squared difference between predicted probability $p_i$ and label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ (lower is better). Class weights change the probabilities a model learns: a classifier trained with weights $w_s,w_b$ on the two classes converges to
\[
\tilde p=\frac{w_s p}{w_s p+w_b(1-p)}
\]
instead of the true $p$, i.e.\ it describes a world in which the classes are re-balanced. With $w_b>w_s$ the raw scores therefore \emph{underestimate} the probability of the signal-like class, which is what the reliability diagram of the Random Forest shows (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_calibration}): at a raw score of $0.25$ the observed signal-like fraction is already $0.58$. An isotonic recalibration~\cite{zadrozny_elkan}, fitted on held-out folds of the unweighted training sample, follows the diagonal and halves the Brier score, from $0.122$ to $0.062$. Most of the gain undoes the re-weighting rather than correcting the model; the ranking, and hence the AUC and any selection based on a score threshold, is unchanged by a monotonic recalibration.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:ml_uncertainty}
The uncertainty of a prediction has two components: \emph{aleatoric}, from the intrinsic overlap of the classes in feature space, which more training data cannot reduce, and \emph{epistemic}, from the limited knowledge of the model parameters, which more training data reduces. Three estimators were compared, each giving an ensemble of $M$ predictions $p_m(x)$ for each candidate and quoting their spread, $\sigma(x)=\big[\frac1M\sum_m (p_m(x)-\bar p(x))^2\big]^{1/2}$: the $M=300$ trees of the Random Forest, each trained on a bootstrap sample; $M=300$ forward passes of the FFNN with dropout kept active at inference (MC dropout), which approximates sampling from a Bayesian posterior over the network weights~\cite{gal_mcdropout}; and a deep ensemble~\cite{deep_ensembles} of $M=10$ FFNNs trained from different random initializations. The mean per-candidate uncertainty is $0.178$ for the Random Forest, $0.095$ for MC dropout ($0.099\pm0.006$ over the ten initializations) and $0.050$ for the deep ensemble (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_uncertainty}), with the same values within $10\%$ for background-like and signal-like candidates.

The three estimators answer different questions, which explains the factor $\sim$3.5 between them. The deep ensemble only varies the initialization, on the same training data; MC dropout also perturbs the network structure; the trees of the Random Forest differ in their training data (bootstrap) and in the features they use, and each tree, of limited depth, is individually a poor and discrete estimator of $p$. Their per-candidate values are correlated but not interchangeable (Spearman correlations $0.41$--$0.57$ between pairs). The Random Forest spread contains both components. It is largest for scores around $0.5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_uncertainty}, left), where the classes overlap and the trees disagree most -- an aleatoric contribution; and it decreases with the size of the training set, from $0.220$ with a quarter of it to $0.204$ with half and $0.179$ with the full set, while the test AUC stays at $0.860$--$0.862$ -- the epistemic contribution, which more data reduces.

\subsection{K-means on the Dalitz Plot}
\label{sec:ml_kmeans}
$K$-means is the unsupervised part of the module: it uses no labels, and the partition of the Dalitz plane is learned from the density of the candidates rather than fixed by hand. As an adaptive alternative to the fixed Dalitz-plot binning of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}, the candidates of the fit window were partitioned with $K$-means~\cite{lloyd_kmeans} on the two standardized Dalitz variables $(m^2_{\mathrm{lo}},m^2_{\mathrm{hi}})$. $K$-means partitions the points $x_i$ into $K$ clusters $C_k$ minimizing the within-cluster sum of squares (inertia) $W=\sum_k\sum_{i\in C_k}\lVert x_i-\bar x_k\rVert^2$, with $\bar x_k$ the cluster centroids; $K$ has to be fixed in advance. The quality of a partition is measured by the silhouette~\cite{silhouette}: for each point, $s_i=(b_i-a_i)/\max(a_i,b_i)$ compares its mean distance $a_i$ to the other points of its cluster with the mean distance $b_i$ to the points of the nearest other cluster, and the average over all points ($-1\le s\le 1$, higher is better) measures how well separated the clusters are. $K=8$ was fixed in advance, as a compromise between resolution and the number of candidates per cluster (at least $1233$), and $K=3$, the silhouette optimum over $K=2$--$12$ ($0.46$, against $0.41$ for $K=8$, a local maximum), was added. Both partitions are stable: repeating the clustering with a different random initialization gives an adjusted Rand index~\cite{hubert_ari} above $0.998$.

The asymmetry of each cluster was compared with the global one as in Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}, $S_k=(A_k-A_{\mathrm{glob}})/\sigma(A_k)$. For $K=8$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_kmeans}), two clusters deviate by more than $3\sigma$ (probability $2\times10^{-4}$ for two or more if the asymmetry were uniform), and the homogeneity test gives $\chi^2=46.4/7$ ($p=7\times10^{-8}$). The most significant one lies at low $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ ($1$--$2.5\,\GeV^2$) and intermediate $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$, with $A_k=-0.127\pm0.017$ ($n=3601$, $S_k=-5.1$); two clusters at high $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ have positive asymmetries, $A_k=+0.020\pm0.020$ ($S_k=+3.2$) and $+0.040\pm0.028$ ($+2.9$). In the signal window ($|m-\mu|<2\sigma$) the same pattern is sharper -- three clusters beyond $3\sigma$, with $S_k=-5.6$, $+4.4$ and $+3.0$, and $\chi^2=62.3/7$ -- so it is not produced by the background. The coarser $K=3$ partition gives $\chi^2=15.2/2$ ($p=5\times10^{-4}$). The adaptive partition therefore confirms the picture of the fixed binning, a negative local asymmetry at low $m^2_{\mathrm{lo}}$ and positive ones at high $m^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$, with the same caveats (no background subtraction, no correction for detection asymmetries).

\subsection{Stability of $\Araw$ under ML Selection}
\label{sec:ml_stability}
The reference (gradient-boosting) score was applied to all candidates of the fit window (on the test set, a cut at $0.5$ keeps $88\%$ of the signal-like and $28\%$ of the background-like candidates, Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_roc}), not only to the labeled ones used in training, and the counting asymmetry was recomputed after cuts on the score from $0.1$ to $0.9$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_threshold}). Since every selected sample is contained in the full one, the expected statistical spread of the difference is $\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{sub}}^2-\sigma_{\mathrm{full}}^2}$, not the uncertainty of either estimate. Over all thresholds, which keep from $18{,}443$ to $2621$ candidates, the shift with respect to the full window ($A_{\mathrm{count}}=-0.0432$) stays within $1.4$ times this spread: the selection does not introduce a detectable bias in the counting asymmetry. Split by magnet polarity at a cut of $0.5$, the counting asymmetries are $-0.060\pm0.010$ (down) and $-0.024\pm0.012$ (up), $2.2\sigma$ apart; the frequentist fit of the same samples, which separates the background, gives $-0.054\pm0.011$ and $-0.032\pm0.013$, $1.3\sigma$ apart (against $0.7\sigma$ for the full sample, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_checks}), so most of the larger difference of the counting estimates comes from the background.

Counting cannot reveal whether the selection deforms the mass spectrum. To test this, the selected sample (score $>0.5$, $15{,}709$ candidates) was refitted with the nominal mass model by both inference methods of this work, the Bayesian fit of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayesian} and the frequentist maximum-likelihood fit of Sec.~\ref{sec:frequentist}, and the three estimates were compared with those of the full sample (Table~\ref{tab:three_methods}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_three}). The asymmetry is stable: all three shifts are within their expected nested-sample spread, and the significance of $A\neq0$ from the profile likelihood (Wilks' theorem~\cite{wilks}) is $5.2\sigma$ in both samples. The selection keeps $92\%$ of the fitted signal and $36\%$ of the background ($N_s=14{,}121$, $N_b=1590$), but this does not improve the precision on $\Araw$ ($0.0087$ against $0.0085$): the background under the peak is already small ($\approx$4\% in the signal window) and the fit separates it from the signal anyway. The mass spectrum, instead, changes. The goodness of fit of the nominal model degrades from $\chi^2=164/88$ to $253/88$ in the Bayesian fit and from $442/388$ to $557/388$ in the frequentist one (with a different binning), and the background shape changes: the slope of the slow exponential grows from $0.0028$ to $0.0071\,\MeV^{-1}$ ($B^+$) and from $0.0016$ to $0.0057\,\MeV^{-1}$ ($B^-$), and the fraction of the steep one from $0.11$ to $0.28$ and from $0.21$ to $0.50$. Two mechanisms can produce this: the classifier could reconstruct the mass from the kaon momenta and sculpt the background (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}), or the selection could change the composition of the background. The first is excluded: the same classifier trained without the nine momentum components (test AUC $0.860$) gives the same degradation ($\chi^2=533/388$). The efficiency of the cut as a function of the mass shows the second (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_mass_cut}): it is $0.89$--$0.90$ at the peak, $\approx0.70$ below $5150\,\MeV$ and $0.06$--$0.13$ above $5375\,\MeV$. The classifier does what it is trained for -- it removes the combinatorial background and keeps genuine $B$ decays, including the partially reconstructed ones and the other non-combinatorial components that the weak labels cannot separate from the signal. In the selected sample these components weigh relatively more, and the nominal model, which has no dedicated component for them, describes it worse, mostly under the peak (the $\chi^2$ of the $\pm4\sigma$ region grows from $75$ to $134$ for 30 bins) and below it. A Crystal Ball signal gives the same degradation ($407\to522$ for 386 degrees of freedom), so it is not due to the signal tails. $\Araw$ is unaffected because it is determined by the peak and the background shape is free in the fit, but a measurement that relied on the background shape, or on a background model taken from the sidebands of the full sample, would be biased by the selection.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
\label{sec:ml_hyper}
The hyperparameters of the Random Forest ($120$ configurations), gradient boosting ($100$) and the FFNN ($168$: seven architectures, four dropout rates, six learning rates) were optimized by repeated cross-validation on the training set (Tables~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams}--\ref{tab:ml_ffnn_search}). The best configurations improve the test AUC over the defaults of Table~\ref{tab:ml_models} by $0.005$ (Random Forest), $0.001$ (gradient boosting) and $0.004$ (FFNN), all well below the test-set uncertainty ($0.012$) and, for the FFNN, comparable with its spread over initializations ($0.005$). For the two tree ensembles the best cross-validated AUC is higher than the test AUC of the same configuration ($0.871$ vs $0.866$ and $0.868$ vs $0.865$), as expected when the maximum is taken over many noisy cross-validation estimates (the ``winner's curse''): the selection favors configurations with a favorable fluctuation, and the cross-validated score of the selected configuration is an optimistic estimate of its performance. The searches show that the defaults are already close to optimal for this problem, so the defaults are used throughout.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several modeling choices and known weaknesses limit how far the results of Secs.~\ref{sec:bayesian}--\ref{sec:ml} can be pushed, and are collected here rather than scattered through the results sections.

On the mass fits (Secs.~\ref{sec:bayesian}--\ref{sec:syst}): no tested signal model reaches $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}\approx1$ (the best, a Crystal Ball, gives $131.0/86$, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_gof}), although $\Araw$ does not depend on the signal shape; the systematic uncertainty, $0.007$, is almost as large as the statistical one; it is dominated by a PID variant that a finer scan identifies as a statistical fluctuation (Sec.~\ref{sec:syst}), so it is conservative, but the combination rule cannot separate the two; the Savage-Dickey Bayes factor of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_factor} relies on a Gaussian approximation of the posterior far in its tail and depends on the prior width, though the conclusion (decisive evidence against $A=0$) is robust; the toy-MC coverage check of Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_toymc} is a closure test and cannot detect model misspecification; and the local Dalitz asymmetries (Secs.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz} and \ref{sec:ml_kmeans}) use a coarse partition, a mass-window cut rather than a proper background subtraction, and no correction for momentum-dependent detection asymmetries.

On the machine-learning side (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml}): the weak labels are imbalanced ($1940$ background-like vs.\ $15{,}130$ signal-like candidates, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_labels}), and the test set contains only $388$ background-like candidates, so differences in test AUC below $\sim$0.01 cannot be resolved; the three uncertainty estimators differ by a factor $\sim$3.5 in scale and measure different sources of variability (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_uncertainty}); and the score-based selection changes the composition of the background, keeping the partially reconstructed and other non-combinatorial $B$ decays that the nominal model does not describe (Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_stability}); removing the momentum features does not help, since the weak labels cannot separate these decays from the signal.

More generally, no detector-level systematic correction (selection efficiency, production asymmetry) is applied anywhere in this work, so $\Araw$ as reported throughout is explicitly \emph{not} a measurement of $\ACP$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}). The published analysis corrects for $A_P+A_D$ with $B^\pm\to J/\psi K^\pm$, $J/\psi\to\mu^+\mu^-$~\cite{lhcb_cpv_prd2023}, which is not part of this record; the $J/\psi\to K^+K^-$ decays inside our sample cannot replace it, since this mode is rare and the $568$ candidates under the $B$ peak in the $J/\psi$ band are dominated by charmless decays ($A_{\mathrm{count}}=-0.165\pm0.041$, a local Dalitz-plot asymmetry rather than a control measurement).

The most natural next steps, in rough priority order, are: correcting $\Araw$ for the production and detection asymmetries with a control channel such as $B^\pm\to J/\psi K^\pm$, $J/\psi\to\mu^+\mu^-$; replacing the fixed-cut local Dalitz asymmetries (both binned, Sec.~\ref{sec:bayes_dalitz}, and $K$-means, Sec.~\ref{sec:ml_kmeans}) with a proper sWeight-based background subtraction; and an explicit model of the partially reconstructed and other non-combinatorial $B$ backgrounds, which would also make an ML-selected sample usable for a fit.

\label{sec:code}
All code is written in Python $\geq$3.12, with \texttt{scikit-learn}~\cite{sklearn} for the classical machine-learning models and \texttt{TensorFlow}/\texttt{Keras}~\cite{tensorflow} for the neural networks. Key package versions: \texttt{uproot 5.7}, \texttt{iminuit 2.32.0}, \texttt{emcee 3.1.6}, \texttt{corner 2.3.0}, \texttt{scikit-learn 1.9}, \texttt{tensorflow 2.21.0}, \texttt{keras 3.15.1}, \texttt{scipy 1.18}, \texttt{numpy 2.4}, \texttt{pandas 3.0}.

The dataset is the CERN Open Data record \href{https://opendata.cern.ch/record/4900}{4900} ("Matter Antimatter Differences -- B meson decays to three hadrons"). The analysis code is available in the accompanying repository, in \texttt{Code\_general/Frequentist/} (frequentist fits and the common systematic study), \texttt{Code\_general/Bayesian/} (nominal model, its checks, and the minimal model), and \texttt{Code\_general/Machine\_Learning/}; the figures of this paper are produced from the saved results of those analyses by the scripts in \texttt{Report/figures/} (\texttt{regen\_mass\_spectrum.py}, \texttt{regen\_figures\_nominal.py}, \texttt{regen\_figures\_ml\_nominal.py}).
